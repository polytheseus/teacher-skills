60 report card comment examples with next steps

Specific comments for ten areas, from reading to secondary subjects, each at three levels: excelling, on track and needs support. Every one follows the same pattern: a strength, the evidence for it, and a next step a parent can help with. Change the evidence to what your student actually did and they're yours.

Updated 23 October 2026 · UK and US spelling · Primary/elementary and secondary/high school

Spelling: (math/maths, behavior/behaviour, quarter/term…)

The formula: strength + evidence + next step. "[Name] has made strong progress in reading, moving from short chapter books to longer novels by choice. Next step: answer 'why' questions about characters' choices." Parents remember the evidence and act on the next step. Leave either out and the comment turns generic.

The examples use [Name] and refer to the student by name rather than he/she, so you can paste them into a spreadsheet and merge names in later (see the SUBSTITUTE formula). Each shows its word and character count, because many school systems cap comment length.

How to write a comment from scratch

PartAsk yourselfExample
StrengthWhat can this student do now that they couldn't before, or do especially well?"[Name] writes with a clear sense of audience."
EvidenceWhich piece of work, moment or result shows it?"Their persuasive letter used rhetorical questions and strong evidence."
Area to developWhat is the one thing holding them back? Describe the skill or habit, not the child."Longer pieces sometimes lose their structure."
Next stepWhat exactly should they do next, and how could home help?"Next step: plan with a quick paragraph outline before writing."

For a student who needs support, lead with a genuine strength and state the concern plainly. Parents should be able to tell what you're worried about; a concern buried under cheerful phrases leads to an awkward conversation at the next meeting.

Next-step bank

A next step should be one specific action, small enough to start tomorrow. Pick one per comment; two at most.

Reading

  • Read aloud for ten minutes a day and talk about one thing that surprised them.
  • Answer "why" and "how" questions about what they read, not only "what happened".
  • Try a genre they usually avoid and compare it with a favourite.

Writing

  • Reread every piece before handing it in to check punctuation and sense.
  • Plan with a quick list or mind map before starting longer writing.
  • Use more precise vocabulary in place of words like "nice" and "good".

Math

  • Practice the 6, 7 and 8 times tables with a short daily game.
  • Estimate first, then check that the answer is reasonable.
  • Explain each step of a method using "because".

Science

  • Make a prediction with a reason before each experiment.
  • Use the topic word mat to include key vocabulary in written answers.
  • Write conclusions that start "The results show that…".

Work habits

  • Start tasks promptly after instructions are given.
  • Try one strategy before asking for help: reread, check an example, ask a partner.
  • Use a planner to record homework deadlines and check it nightly.

Social and behavior

  • Use our agreed signal to share ideas at the right moment.
  • Practice compromising in group work by taking turns or voting.
  • Share at least one idea in every whole-class discussion.

Common questions

How long should a report card comment be?

Follow your school's limit. Most comments run 40–90 words, roughly 250–550 characters. Many school information systems (MIS/SIS) cap comments at a fixed number of characters, so check yours before you write thirty.

Can I copy these word for word?

Use them as models. Swap in real evidence from your notes and change the wording to sound like you. If several students get the same comment, parents compare notes at the school gate.

How do I write a comment for a student with SEN or an IEP?

Write about the learning you see ("uses a word bank to build longer sentences"), not the diagnosis or provision. If the report has to mention support arrangements, follow your school's wording and check with your SENCO or case manager.

Should a comment mention problems in class?

If it affects learning, yes, described factually ("talks during independent work, which slows progress") with what's being done about it. Serious incidents belong in direct conversation with families, not a first appearance on a report.

Third person or second person?

Most schools use third person ("[Name] has…") for reports to parents. Some secondary schools address the student ("You have…"). To convert these examples, replace "[Name]" with "you" and fix the verbs.
