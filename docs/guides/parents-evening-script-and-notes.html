Parents' evening script and notes template for teachers

Thirty appointments, five minutes each, and every parent wants to know the same three things: how is my child doing, what should they work on, and how can I help? Here's a script that answers all three inside a 5-minute slot, a table of vague targets turned into ones a child can act on, phrases for the hard conversations, a follow-up message, and a free notes builder that turns one line per student into a printable sheet.

Updated 27 October 2026 · UK parents' evenings and US parent-teacher conferences · About a 7-minute read

The short version: for each student, write one line before the evening: one specific strength, one specific next step, one thing parents can do at home. In the slot, say them in that order after a warm welcome and a sentence on where the child is now, then hand over: "How does she feel about it at home?" Keep your part to about half the slot, because parents who get to talk leave happier. Afterwards, send a three-line follow-up. The notes builder below does the script, the slot times and the follow-up for you, and flags targets like "try harder".

The 5-part script (fits a 5-minute slot)

PartTimeWhat to say
1. Welcome20 sec"Thanks for coming. I teach Amira maths and I really enjoy having her in the class." Use the child's name early: some parents are seeing five teachers and need to know who you are.
2. One strength40 secSpecific, with evidence. "She explains her method out loud in group work. Last week she talked her table through the fractions task." Show the book or piece of work if you have it.
3. Where they are1 minThe level, grade or reading band your school uses, and how you know. "She's working at the expected standard, based on the last two assessments."
4. One next step1 minThe single thing that would make the biggest difference, in words a child could act on tomorrow. "Show every step of working on 2-mark questions: she's losing marks for right answers with no method."
5. Home30 secOne small, realistic thing. "Ask her to teach you one question from her homework each week."
Then: their turnthe rest"How does she feel about maths at home? Is there anything you'd like to ask me?" Then close: "I'll check in on the working by half term."

For a 10-minute primary slot or a 15–20 minute US conference, keep the same order and spend the extra time on part 3 (show two or three pieces of work) and part 6 (listen). Don't add more targets: parents remember one, two at most.

Vague targets, fixed

The most common parents' evening complaint is "the teacher said she was lovely, but I still don't know what to do." These are the targets that cause it, and what to say instead:

VagueSpecific (a child can act on it)
Try harder / more effortFinish the last two questions of each homework, even if you're unsure, and circle the ones you guessed.
Focus more in classStart the starter task within two minutes of sitting down.
Be more confidentPut a hand up for one question in each lesson; I'll make sure the first one is one he knows.
Improve her writingUse a capital letter and a full stop in every sentence, then check them before handing in.
Revise more15 minutes of self-quizzing with the knowledge organiser, three times a week, covering the answers and checking.
Participate moreShare one idea in each group task, even a half-finished one.
Keep it upTry the challenge question first, then go back to the main task.
Behave betterStay in his seat during independent work; he gets 5 minutes' free choice when he does.

The same goes for praise. "Lovely and polite" is nice to hear, but parents can't picture it. "Held the door and helped a new student find the library on his first day" is the same praise with something to remember.

Phrases for the hard conversations

  • The child is behind. Say it plainly, with evidence, then go straight to the plan: "At the moment he's working towards the expected standard in reading, based on his last two assessments. The thing that will help most is reading aloud for 10 minutes a day, and he's in a small group with Mrs K three times a week for it." If you soften it too much, parents find out in July and ask why nobody said.
  • Behaviour. Describe what happened, not the child: "Three times this half term he's left his seat during tests" rather than "he's disruptive". Then: "What works at home when he gets restless?" Parents often know the answer.
  • The quiet one. "She's one of the students I'd like to hear more from. Her written work shows she knows more than she says." Then one small, safe step (part 5 of the script).
  • The high achiever. Parents still want a next step. "He's working above the expected standard. To stretch him, I'd like him to try the challenge question first, and explain his reasoning in writing, not just get the answer."
  • The upset or angry parent. Let them finish. "I can see this has been really worrying." Then facts and one next step, and if it needs more than the slot: "This deserves more than five minutes. Can we book a proper meeting this week, with Mr J from pastoral?" Write down what was agreed.
  • SEN or a possible need. Don't diagnose ("I think he's dyslexic"). Describe what you see, and say who can look at it: "He finds copying from the board hard and his spelling hasn't moved this term. I'd like to talk to our SENCO about it, if that's OK with you." In the US, the equivalent is the school's intervention or special-education team.
  • You're running late. "I'm sorry, I'm running 10 minutes behind. I'll keep to time with you, and I'll email you the full picture tomorrow." Then actually do the follow-up.
  • Parents who didn't come. Send the same follow-up message anyway. They are often the parents who most need it.

If a parent or child tells you something worrying (about harm, neglect, or someone's safety at home), stop the appointment from being "just another slot". Listen, don't promise to keep it secret, write down what was said in their words as soon as you can, and pass it to your designated safeguarding lead (DSL) the same day, following your school's procedure (in England this is set out in the Department for Education's Keeping children safe in education guidance). In the US, follow your district's mandated-reporter procedure. Never put it in an AI chat or a follow-up email.

What to have on the table

  • Your one-line notes (or the printed sheet from the builder), in appointment order.
  • The child's book or two pieces of work, marked, so you can show the strength and the next step.
  • The current level, grade or reading band, and the last assessment result.
  • Attendance and homework completion if your school shares them at parents' evening.
  • A clock where you can see it and the parent can't, and a notepad for anything you promise to follow up.

Don't leave the sheet on the desk between appointments: the next parent can read it upside down. That's another reason to keep sensitive details off it entirely.

Using ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude to prepare

AI tools are good at turning your rough notes ("AB, gd at explaining, loses marks no working") into a script and a specific target. Check your school's AI policy first, use initials only, and leave out anything sensitive (SEN details, health, family circumstances, safeguarding). Then paste this:

I'm a [Year 5 / Year 9 maths / 3rd grade] teacher preparing for parents' evening. Slots are [5] minutes. For each student below (initials only), write:
1. One specific strength, using my example (don't invent examples).
2. One next step a child could act on tomorrow (no "try harder", "focus more", "be more confident").
3. One small thing parents can do at home, under 15 minutes.
4. A 3-line follow-up message I can send afterwards.
Keep my facts exactly as I wrote them. If my note is too vague to write a specific next step, ask me instead of guessing.

[AB: explains method well in group work (fractions task last week); loses marks for no working on 2-mark questions]
[CD: ...]

Read every line before you use it. The AI doesn't know the child: if it writes a strength you didn't give it, delete it. Our guide to using ChatGPT without sharing student data shows a simple code system for this.

The follow-up message

Send it within a couple of days, through the channel your school uses. Three lines are enough, and they turn a 5-minute chat into something the family can act on:

Dear [Parent name],

Thank you for coming to parents' evening to talk about [Student]. A quick summary of what we agreed:

- Going well: explaining her method out loud in group work.
- Next step: show every step of working on 2-mark questions.
- At home: ask her to teach you one question from her homework each week.

I'll check in on the next step by half term. Please get in touch through [school channel] if you have any questions before then.

Best wishes,
[Your name]

Common questions

What should a teacher say at parents' evening?

A welcome, one specific strength with an example, where the child is now and how you know, one specific next step, and one thing parents can do at home. Then ask the parents a question and listen. Keep your part to about half the slot.

How long is a parents' evening appointment?

It varies by school. UK secondary slots are often 5 minutes per subject teacher and primary slots often 10; US parent-teacher conferences are often 15–20 minutes. The script above works for all of them: only parts 3 and 6 stretch.

Should I mention behaviour if the parent doesn't ask?

If it's affecting learning, yes, briefly and with facts, and ideally not as the first thing you say. If it's serious or ongoing, parents shouldn't be hearing it for the first time at parents' evening: raise it before, by message or a call, and use the slot to agree the next step.

What do I do when parents ask about other children?

"I can only talk about Sam, but I can tell you what we're doing about it in class." Never share another child's information, even if the parent already knows the name.

Can I use ChatGPT to write parents' evening notes?

Within your school's AI policy, with initials instead of names and nothing sensitive in the prompt. Give it your own facts and ask it to turn them into a script and specific targets, then check every line.
