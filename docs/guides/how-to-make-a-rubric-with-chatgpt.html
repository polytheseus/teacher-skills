How to make a rubric with ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude (that isn't vague)

Ask an AI for a rubric and you'll usually get a neat grid that falls apart the first time you mark with it: "excellent use of evidence", "good use of evidence", "some use of evidence". Here's a prompt that avoids that, a real vague rubric fixed row by row, a single-point version, a student "I can" version, and a free checker you can paste any rubric into.

Updated 26 October 2026 · UK and US · About an 8-minute read

The short version: give the AI the task, the learning objective, the age group and how many levels you want (4 is a good default). Tell it to write the target level first, to describe only things a marker can see in the work, and to change what the student does between levels rather than adding "some", "most" or "all". Keep it to 3–6 criteria. Then do a 2-minute calibration test: picture a real piece of work at each level. If two levels sound the same, rewrite them. Paste the result into the checker below to catch the vague words.

Why AI rubrics come out vague

Most rubrics online, and so most of what the AI learned from, are built as an adverb ladder: the same sentence four times with a different quality word. They look finished, but they don't help you mark or help a student improve:

  • Two markers disagree. Is three quotations "some" evidence or "most"? Nobody can tell from the rubric.
  • Students can't use it. "Make your argument more insightful" isn't something a 13-year-old can act on.
  • It marks the wrong things. Neatness, length and "effort" rows creep in and outweigh the learning.
  • Two things share a row. "Organisation and grammar": a well-organised essay full of errors fits no level.

The fix is the same every time: descriptors that say what's on the page, and levels that change what the student does.

Step 1: Pick the type

TypeBest forTrade-off
Analytic (criteria × levels grid)Essays, projects, lab reports; detailed feedback and moderationSlower to mark
Holistic (one scale)Timed writing, performances, quick markingLess diagnostic
Single-point (target level only)Drafts, formative feedback, self- and peer-assessmentNeeds written comments
Checklist (yes / no)Procedures and requirements: title, bibliography, units on graphsDoesn't measure quality

A useful split: put requirements (word count, headings, references present) in a checklist, and keep the rubric for quality.

Step 2: Paste this prompt

It works in ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot, including free plans. Change the parts in capitals.

Write a marking rubric.
Task: [WHAT STUDENTS PRODUCE, e.g. a 600-word persuasive essay on school uniform]
Age / year group: [e.g. Year 8 (UK) / Grade 7 (US)]
Learning objective or standard: [PASTE THE EXACT WORDING. If you are not sure of
a standard's wording, ask me to paste it; don't invent official text.]
Type: [analytic / single-point / holistic]. Levels: [4: Beginning, Developing,
Proficient, Exemplary]. Points: [e.g. 4-3-2-1 per criterion, Argument x2].

Rules:
1. 3-6 criteria, each tracing to the objective. One skill per criterion.
   Requirements like length, title or neatness go in a separate checklist.
2. Write the target level (Proficient) first, then the levels above and below.
3. Every descriptor describes something a marker can see in the work.
   No bare judgment words (good, excellent, effective, insightful, appropriate).
4. Levels differ by WHAT the student does, not only by some/most/all or
   adverbs. Use a count only where the count matters ("at least two").
5. Lower levels say what the work does show first ("gives an opinion but
   doesn't yet support it"), not only what's missing.
6. A typical secure piece for this age lands at Proficient, and Exemplary
   is reachable by a strong student of this age.

Output:
- The rubric as a table (criteria rows x level columns) with points.
- A student-friendly "I can..." version of the Proficient level.
- A calibration check: one sentence describing a piece of work at each level
  for this task. If two levels sound alike, fix them before showing me.
- One feedback comment stem per level.

Got a rubric you already use? Paste it in instead with "Diagnose this rubric against the rules above, list the 3–5 biggest problems, then rewrite it. Keep my criteria and labels unless they're the problem."

Step 3: Fix the vague rows (a real example)

Here's the kind of rubric you get from a one-line prompt ("make a rubric for a persuasive essay"), next to the fixed version. Year 8 / Grade 7, objective: write an argument that supports a claim with clear reasons and relevant evidence.

Before

CriteriaExcellent (4)Good (3)Fair (2)Poor (1)
Ideas and argumentExcellent, insightful argumentGood argumentSome argumentPoor argument
Use of evidenceUses lots of relevant evidenceUses some relevant evidenceUses little relevant evidenceNo evidence
Organization and grammarVery well organized with no errorsWell organized with few errorsSomewhat organized with some errorsNot organized, many errors
NeatnessVery neatNeatSomewhat messyMessy

What's wrong: every row is an adverb ladder; "argument" and "evidence" levels can't be told apart without guessing; organisation and grammar share a row; neatness is a quarter of the mark on a writing task; the lowest levels only say what's missing.

After

CriterionBeginning (1)Developing (2)Proficient (3)Exemplary (4)
ClaimStates an opinion on the topic, but it changes during the essay.States a claim in the introduction and keeps to it.States a precise claim in the introduction that each body paragraph supports.States a precise claim and acknowledges a counterclaim, explaining why the claim still holds.
Use of evidence (×2)Gives opinions without supporting evidence, or the evidence is unrelated to the claim.Includes evidence that relates to the claim, but doesn't explain how it supports it.Supports each main point with relevant evidence and explains in their own words how it supports the claim.Selects the most convincing evidence, explains it, and answers a piece of evidence that could support the other side.
OrganisationIdeas appear in the order they occurred to the writer, without paragraphs.Uses paragraphs, but a paragraph covers more than one point.Each paragraph makes one point, opens with a topic sentence and links back to the claim.Paragraphs build on each other, with transitions that show how each point strengthens the argument.
Accuracy of writingErrors in spelling or sentence boundaries make sentences hard to follow.Meaning is clear, but run-on sentences or comma splices appear in more than one paragraph.Sentences are correctly punctuated; spelling errors are rare and don't affect meaning.Varies sentence structure for effect (e.g. a short sentence after a long one) with accurate punctuation, including semicolons or colons where used.

Total 20 points (evidence counts double). Neatness, title and word count moved to a checklist that's ticked, not scored. Notice that every level describes something you can point to in the essay, and each step up adds a new thing the student does.

Borderline work: if a piece meets all of Proficient and some of Exemplary, award Proficient and say which Exemplary feature to aim for next. That one rule settles most marking disagreements.

Step 4: The single-point version (fastest to write)

For drafts and feedback lessons, keep only the target column and leave space either side:

Not yet: what to work onTarget (Proficient)Beyond: what went further
I state a precise claim in my introduction and every body paragraph supports it.
I back up each main point with relevant evidence and explain, in my own words, how it supports my claim.
Each paragraph makes one point, opens with a topic sentence and links back to my claim.
My sentences are correctly punctuated, and spelling mistakes don't get in the way of my meaning.

The middle column doubles as the student-friendly "I can" checklist. Hand it out before students start writing, not just with the marks.

Step 5: Do the calibration test

Before you use a rubric, spend two minutes on this (or ask the AI to do it, as the prompt above does):

  • Picture a real piece at each level. Think of an actual student's essay from last year. Can you say which cell it lands in for every row, without hesitating? If you hesitate between two cells, they're too similar.
  • Check the middle. A typical secure piece for the age group should land at Proficient, not Exemplary.
  • Check the top is reachable. If no student you've taught would hit Exemplary, it's describing a different age group.
  • Check with a colleague. Mark two pieces separately with the rubric, then compare. Disagreements show you which cells need rewording. This is also the best way to moderate across a department.
  • Check the points. Add them up and test a mixed profile (strong evidence, weak organisation). Does the total feel right?

Paste a rubric and press Check. Put the level names in the first row and one criterion per row after that. It reads a Markdown table (what ChatGPT outputs), rows copied straight from Google Docs, Sheets or Word, or CSV. It runs entirely in your browser, and nothing you paste is sent or stored.

It flags judgment words with nothing a marker can see, amount words with no count, levels that only differ by adverbs, two skills in one row, rows that mark presentation instead of learning, empty or one-word cells, and too many or too few criteria. Getting it into Google Classroom, Canvas or a document

  • Google Docs or Word: ask the AI "give me the rubric as a table", then copy the table straight in. If it pastes as text with | characters, ask for "tab-separated values" instead, paste into Google Sheets, then copy the cells into your document.
  • Google Classroom: open the assignment and choose Rubric → Create rubric, then paste each criterion title and level description in. Google's help says the Import from Sheets option only accepts rubrics that were created and exported from Classroom, so build it once, then export it to reuse with other classes or share with colleagues.
  • Canvas: rubrics can be built in the rubric editor; institutions using Canvas's newer rubrics can also import from a CSV in Canvas's own template. Check your school's Canvas help pages for the template, because the column layout is specific.
  • Points-free? If your school reports in bands or comments, ask the AI to drop the points and add a feedback comment stem per level instead.

Student work and privacy. Building a rubric needs no student data at all. If you go on to paste student work into an AI to mark against the rubric, follow your school's AI policy first, remove names and anything identifying, and treat any suggested level as a draft for you to confirm. Common questions

Can ChatGPT make a rubric?

Yes, and quickly, but a one-line prompt gives you an adverb ladder. Give it the task, objective, age and levels, and the rules in the prompt above (observable descriptors, levels that change what the student does). Then check it with the calibration test.

How many levels should a rubric have?

Four is a good default: enough to show progress, few enough that markers agree, and no neutral middle that becomes the "safe" mark. If your school uses fixed bands (e.g. working towards / expected / greater depth), use those.

Should I use official standard wording?

Use the exact wording from your curriculum, exam board or state standard, pasted in yourself. AI tools sometimes paraphrase or invent standard text and codes, so don't let it supply them.

What is a single-point rubric?

One column describing the target for each criterion, with blank space for "not yet" and "beyond" comments. It's quick to write and good for drafts and self-assessment, but it relies on your written comments.

Can I import an AI-made rubric into Google Classroom?

Google's help says Classroom only imports rubrics that were created and exported from Classroom, so the dependable route is to create it in the assignment and paste each criterion in. After that you can export and reuse it.
