Report card comments for math: 200+ examples by grade and strand, and a free comment builder

Math comments are the ones that come out vaguest: "Maya is doing well in math" tells a family nothing they can't see from the grade. This guide gives you comments for every main strand (facts, place value, fractions, word problems, explaining thinking, geometry, data and more) for grades K–2, 3–5 and 6–8, each with a strength, an on-track line, a next step and a home tip. There are also 20 rewrites of phrases to avoid, six complete comments, and a free builder that writes and checks each one.

Updated 29 October 2026 · Grades K–8, with UK (maths) wording · About an 8-minute read

The short version: a good math comment names the skill, not just the subject. Open with one real strength in a strand ("knows her times tables by heart and uses them in division"). Then say what's on track, give one next step and how class is working on it ("comparing fractions, with fraction strips and number lines"), and end with one small thing to try at home ("talk about fractions when cooking"). Skip labels like "careless" or "not a math person", and don't compare students with each other. The builder below puts it together for any grade band and checks the wording.

The pattern: skill, next step, home tip

PartWhat it doesExample
1. A specific strengthNames the skill, so the family knows what "good at math" means"Leo knows his times tables by heart and uses them confidently in division."
2. On track (optional)Reassures the family about the part that's going fine"He shows some steps in his work and is learning to include each one, which is right on track for this point in the year."
3. Next step and how class helpsOne thing to aim for, with the support already in place"Our next step is comparing fractions, which we're practicing with fraction strips and number lines."
4. Home tipOne small, concrete activity that isn't a worksheet"At home, you could use fractions when cooking or sharing food."
5. CloseKeeps the door open"Please get in touch if you have any questions about Leo's progress in math."

A moment you noticed beats any stock phrase: "I was impressed when Leo showed the class two ways to work out 25 x 12" tells the family you know their child. One per comment is plenty.

Is it on track? Facts expectations by grade

"On track" should mean something. For number facts, the standards are specific, so it's worth checking before you write "right on track":

US (Common Core)England (national curriculum)
Grade 1: fluent with addition and subtraction within 10 (1.OA.C.6)Year 2: recall addition and subtraction facts to 20 fluently; recall the 2, 5 and 10 times tables
Grade 2: know from memory all sums of two one-digit numbers by the end of the year (2.OA.B.2)Year 3: recall the 3, 4 and 8 times tables
Grade 3: know from memory all products of two one-digit numbers by the end of the year (3.OA.C.7)Year 4: recall multiplication and division facts up to 12 x 12; the multiplication tables check is in June

States and districts that don't use the Common Core have their own standards (for example, Texas uses the TEKS), so check yours. The standard codes are for you, not the comment: families don't know what 3.OA.C.7 means, so write "knows all the times tables to 10 x 10 by heart" instead.

Middle school math: grades, missing work and the student reading it

  • The grade says how; the comment says why. If a B- is mostly down to two missing assignments rather than understanding, say so, and say what fixes it: which assignments, by when, under your actual late-work or retake policy.
  • Write so the student can act on it. From about grade 6, students read their own report cards. "Showing each step, so you can spot your own mistakes" is something a 12-year-old can do; "needs to be more careful" isn't.
  • Name the unit. Middle school math moves fast. "Solving two-step equations" or "percentage change" tells the family what to ask about at home.
  • Point to help that exists. Office hours, a math lab, a homework club, the online textbook's videos. Families often don't know they exist.

UK maths reports

Switch the builder to UK for maths, practise and "term", and the grade bands show Years 1–3, 4–6 and 7–9. Use your school's own attainment language (many primaries report "working towards", "at" or "greater depth" against the expected standard, but write those words in full rather than WTS, EXS or GDS). Leave out set or group names: sets change, and families read "bottom set" as a label. In Year 4, pupils in England take the multiplication tables check in June: 25 questions, six seconds each, with no pass mark. A times-tables next step in Year 3 or 4 is the most useful home tip you can give, because it's the one families most often ask about.

Home tips that families actually use

Families are far more likely to try something that fits into ordinary life than a worksheet. Every home tip in this guide is that kind of thing: fact games in the car, fractions while cooking, estimating at the shops, reading a clock with hands, a tally chart of cars going past. One tip per comment, tied to the next step, is enough. If a parent tells you they were "never any good at math" themselves, a gentle line at a parent conference helps more than anything on the report: praising effort and strategies ("you tried two ways") works whatever their own math was like.

Common questions

What should a report card comment for math include?

One specific strength (the skill, not just "good at math"), what's on track, one next step with how class is working on it, and one small thing to try at home. Two or three strands is enough; the grade boxes cover the rest.

How do you write a positive math comment for a student who finds math hard?

Find a real strength in a strand or a habit: careful measuring, explaining a method to a partner, keeping going on a tricky problem. Say it specifically, then give one next step and the support in place. If the student is well below grade level, make sure the family has heard it from you before the report goes home.

What words should I avoid in math report card comments?

Labels such as careless, lazy or sloppy; fixed-ability lines such as "bad at math", "not a math person" or "not her strong suit"; guesses at feelings such as "math anxiety"; set or group names; comparisons with other students; teacher jargon such as decompose, regroup or algorithm; and "always" and "never". Describe what the student does and what helps instead.

What is a good math home tip for parents?

Something short that fits into everyday life and matches the next step: fact games in the car, fractions while cooking, estimating at the shops, reading a clock with hands, or asking "how did you work that out?" during homework. A few minutes a few times a week is plenty.

Can I use ChatGPT to write math report card comments?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names, diagnoses, support plans and family details. Give it short notes per student with a code instead of a name, ask for the strength, next step and home tip pattern, and read every comment before it goes on a report.
