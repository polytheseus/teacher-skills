Preschool progress report comments: 100+ examples for pre-K, and a free comment builder

A preschool report is often the first written report a family ever gets about their child, and it's about a three- or four-year-old who may only just have learned to say goodbye at the door. It needs to be warm, specific and honest, with no labels and no jargon. Here are 102 comments for every common preschool area at three levels, kind wording for the hard ones, a home tip for each area, a free builder that writes and checks each comment, and a section on the UK progress check at age 2.

Updated 28 October 2026 · US preschool and pre-K, with UK nursery and the 2-year check · About an 8-minute read

The short version: each comment does four things. One or two specific strengths ("plays happily with friends and takes turns"), one next step and how you're working on it ("our next step is following two-step directions, which we're practicing with listening games"), one thing the family can do at home ("give two-step jobs: get your shoes, then find your coat") and a warm close. Write about this child's own progress, use everyday words instead of domain names, and never put a worry on paper before you've talked to the family. The comment builder below puts it together and flags the words to avoid.

The preschool comment formula

PartWhat it doesExample
1. Opening or momentShows you know the child"I loved seeing Rosa count all the ducks in our pond book."
2. StrengthsOne or two areas, named specifically"Rosa plays happily with friends, takes turns and shares ideas for games."
3. Next stepOne area, framed as what comes next, plus what you're doing"Our next step is listening at group time and following two-step directions, which we're practicing with listening games like Simon Says."
4. Home tipOne small, concrete activity"At home, you could give two-step jobs ('get your shoes, then find your coat')."
5. CloseWarm, forward-looking"I'm looking forward to the rest of the year with Rosa."

Two or three areas is plenty. The checklist or rating scale on your report already covers the rest; the comment is where a family finds out what their child is like at school and what to do next.

What areas a pre-K report covers

Use your own program's checklist first. Most US programs follow their state's early learning standards, and Head Start programs use the Head Start Early Learning Outcomes Framework (ELOF), which sets out what young children should know and be able to do in five broad central domains:

ELOF central domainWhat parents would call itAreas in this guide
Approaches to LearningHow they play and learn: curiosity, focus, sticking with thingsCuriosity, attention
Social and Emotional DevelopmentFriends, feelings, settling in, doing things for themselvesPlaying with others, feelings, settling in, independence
Language and LiteracyTalking, listening, stories, their name and letters, rhymes, drawingSix areas
CognitionCounting, shapes and sorting, exploring and asking whyCounting, shapes, exploring
Perceptual, Motor, and Physical DevelopmentRunning and climbing, scissors and crayonsMoving, hand skills

Two things follow for comments. First, the domain names are for you, not for families: "approaches to learning" or "fine motor" means little to most parents, so write what the child does ("uses scissors carefully"). Second, preschool skills grow at very different rates, and the year has only just started. In the fall, "is learning to" and "is starting to" are normal, honest descriptions, not warnings. Save the rating boxes for your program's checkpoints.

Hard comments, written kindly

The comments that take longest are the ones about a child who bites, cries every morning, or isn't talking much yet. Families of a three-year-old read them very closely. Say what the child is learning, pair it with what you're doing and what helps, and keep anything that needs a real conversation off the page.

UK nursery and pre-school reports

In England, nurseries, pre-schools and childminders follow the EYFS statutory framework (the current versions apply from September 2026). It has three prime areas (communication and language; physical development; personal, social and emotional development) and four specific areas (literacy; mathematics; understanding the world; expressive arts and design), plus three characteristics of effective teaching and learning: playing and exploring, active learning, and creating and thinking critically.

Apart from the progress check at age 2 (below) and the EYFS profile at the end of Reception, the framework doesn't set a format for nursery reports. It also says ongoing assessment shouldn't mean piles of paperwork: there's no requirement to keep written records of day-to-day observations (paragraph 2.5). So a nursery report can be short and warm. Switch the builder to UK nursery / pre-school for nursery, maths, term, Reception and UK spelling. Development Matters gives the non-statutory picture of how children typically develop, but don't quote its age bands or statements to parents: describe what the child does.

The progress check at age 2: what to write

The EYFS framework (paragraphs 2.6 to 2.10) says that when a child is aged between two and three, practitioners must review their progress and give parents a short written summary of their development in the three prime areas. The summary must:

  • highlight the areas where the child is progressing well;
  • highlight areas where some additional support might be needed;
  • focus particularly on any area where there's a concern the child may have a developmental delay;
  • describe the activities and strategies the setting will use to address any concerns, planned with the parents (and the SENCO or health professionals where appropriate).

You must also discuss with parents how the summary can support learning at home and agree with them when it's most useful to give it. Encourage them to share it with their health visitor (ideally it informs the health visitor's two-year review), and get their consent before you share it with anyone else yourself. If a child moves settings between two and three, the setting where they spend most time usually does the check.

That's the one place a written summary should name a concern. The way to do it is to describe what you see and what you'll do, not to label it: "Theo is working on using more words and putting two words together. How we'll help: …". Talk it through with the parents before it's written down, and leave diagnoses to the professionals. The government's non-statutory progress check at age 2 guidance has more detail and examples.

Common questions

What should I write in a preschool progress report comment?

One or two specific things the child does well, one next step and how you're working on it, one small activity the family can do at home, and a warm close. Describe this child's own progress in plain words, and mention a real moment if you can.

How do I write a preschool comment for a child who is finding things hard?

Start with a real strength, then describe the skill as a next step: 'is still learning to' or 'is working on' instead of 'can't', 'won't' or 'is behind'. Say what you're doing and give one home idea. If you're worried about a child's development, talk to the family in person first and never suggest a diagnosis in writing.

What areas should a pre-K report cover?

Use your program's checklist. Many US programs follow their state's early learning standards, and Head Start uses the Early Learning Outcomes Framework, which has five central domains: approaches to learning, social and emotional development, language and literacy, cognition (math and science) and perceptual, motor and physical development. In England, the EYFS has three prime areas and four specific areas.

What is the progress check at age 2?

In England, the EYFS says that when a child is aged between two and three, practitioners must review their progress and give parents a short written summary of their development in the three prime areas: personal, social and emotional development, communication and language, and physical development. It must say where the child is progressing well, where some extra support might be needed, and what the setting will do about any concerns.

How long should a preschool progress report comment be?

Follow your program's format. Three to six short sentences is usually enough: a strength, a next step with what you're doing, a home tip and a close. Families read every word of a preschool report, so short and specific beats long and general.

Can I use ChatGPT to write preschool progress report comments?

Yes, within your program's policy, if you leave out names and private details. Give it short notes for each child using a code, ask for a fixed pattern (strength, next step, home tip), then read every comment, add the names back and check it sounds like you.
