Report card comments for reading and writing: 240 examples by grade and skill, and a free comment builder

"Maya is a good reader" is the comment families read most often and learn least from. This guide gives you reading, writing and speaking comments for grades K–2, 3–5 and 6–8, split by skill (phonics, reading aloud, comprehension, reading between the lines, spelling, sentences, paragraphs, ideas, editing and more). Each skill has two strengths, two on-track lines, a next step and a home tip. There are also 22 rewrites of phrases to avoid, six complete comments, and a free builder that writes and checks each one.

Updated 29 October 2026 · Grades K–8 (ELA), with UK English wording · About an 8-minute read

The short version: name the skill, not the subject or the reading level. Open with one real strength ("reads between the lines, working out how characters feel and why"). Say what's on track, give one next step and how class is working on it ("organizing writing into paragraphs, with planning frames"), and end with one small thing to try at home ("ask her to tell you the plan for a story before she writes it"). Leave out labels like "reluctant reader" or "poor speller", reading-level letters families can't decode, and comparisons with other students. The builder below puts it together for any grade band and checks the wording.

The pattern: skill, next step, home tip

PartWhat it doesExample
1. A specific strengthNames the skill, so "good reader" means something"Amara reads between the lines, working out how characters feel and why."
2. On track (optional)Reassures the family about the part that's going fine"She spells well in spelling tests and is starting to carry that into her writing, which is right on track for this point in the year."
3. Next step and how class helpsOne thing to aim for, with the support already in place"Our next step is organizing writing into paragraphs, each with one main idea, which we're practicing with planning frames."
4. Home tipOne small, concrete activity that isn't a worksheet"At home, you could ask Amara to tell you the plan for a story before writing it."
5. CloseKeeps the door open"Please get in touch if you have any questions about Amara's reading and writing."

A moment you noticed beats any stock phrase: "I was impressed when Amara explained why the main character lied, using three clues from the chapter" tells the family you know their child. One per comment is plenty. Reading and writing are often one grade box but two very different skill sets, so it's fine to pick one reading strand and one writing strand.

Reading levels, scores and checks: what to say instead

Reading is where report jargon is worst. A family who reads "Level M", "520L", "DRA 18" or "orange book band" has no idea whether to worry. Unless your school asks you to report the level, describe what the student reads instead, and if you must report it, explain the scale once.

Instead ofSay what it means
Reading at Level J (guided reading)Reads simple chapter books on her own and is working on reading them with expression.
Lexile 520LReads books with short chapters confidently and is starting to try longer ones.
Reading age 6 years 9 monthsReads short, familiar books smoothly and is learning to sound out longer words.
Below benchmark on DIBELSIs learning to read short words more quickly; we're practicing with daily phonics games. (If this is a real concern, talk to the family before the report goes home.)

US: the Common Core reading standards for grades 1–5 each include reading "with sufficient accuracy and fluency to support comprehension" (RF.1.4 to RF.5.4), so fluency is a fair thing to comment on at every elementary grade. The codes are for you, not the comment: write "reads aloud smoothly and with expression" instead of RF.3.4. States that don't use the Common Core have their own standards, so check yours.

England: most children take the phonics screening check in Year 1: 40 words, in two sections of 20, read aloud to a teacher, some of them made-up words. Children who don't meet the expected standard take it again in Year 2. In Year 6, the key stage 2 tests include reading and a grammar, punctuation and spelling test, while writing is assessed by the teacher. If a comment mentions any of these, say what the result means for the child, not the score.

Middle school ELA: essays, independent reading and the student reading it

  • Name the unit and the skill. "Argument writing: supporting claims with evidence" or "analyzing how the writer builds tension in Holes" tells the family what to ask about at the dinner table.
  • Write so the student can act on it. From about grade 6, students read their own report cards. "Explaining how each quotation proves your point" is something a 12-year-old can do; "needs to develop analysis" isn't.
  • Separate the work from the habits. If the grade is mostly down to missing independent reading logs or a late essay, say so, and say what fixes it under your actual late-work policy.
  • Point to help that exists. A writing center, the school library, office hours, the class page with model essays. Families often don't know they exist.

Home tips that families actually use

The most useful reading home tip is still the simplest: a few minutes of reading together most days, with the child choosing some of the books. Every home tip in this guide fits into ordinary life: sounding out words on cereal packets, taking turns reading pages at bedtime, retelling a chapter in three sentences, a dinner-table debate with three reasons, reading an essay aloud to spot one improvement. One tip per comment, tied to the next step, is enough. Avoid tips that sound like homework for the parent ("please complete the reading log nightly"); if the reading log matters, mention it in a note home rather than the report.

Common questions

What should a reading report card comment include?

One specific reading skill the student does well (sounding out longer words, retelling a story in order, finding clues about how a character feels), whether the rest is on track, one next step with how class is working on it, and one small thing to try at home, such as ten minutes of reading together. Name the skill, not the reading level.

What should a writing report card comment include?

The same pattern, with a writing skill: ideas and detail, organising writing into paragraphs, sentences and punctuation, spelling, handwriting or editing. Pick one strength and one next step; a comment that lists every writing skill gives the family nothing to act on.

Should I put a student's reading level in the report card comment?

Usually not on its own. Letters, Lexile numbers, DRA scores and book bands mean little to most families. Say what kind of books the student reads comfortably and what comes next, and if your school reports a level, explain the scale once alongside it.

What words should I avoid in reading and writing report card comments?

Labels such as reluctant reader, poor speller or lazy; fixed-ability lines such as "not a natural writer" or "not a reader"; guesses at feelings such as "hates writing"; verdicts such as messy or illegible; group names and comparisons; teacher jargon such as digraphs, decoding or fronted adverbials; and "always" and "never". Describe what the student does and what helps instead.

Can I use ChatGPT to write reading and writing report card comments?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names, diagnoses, support plans, reading-intervention details and family information. Give it short notes per student with a code instead of a name, ask for the strength, next step and home tip pattern, and read every comment before it goes on a report.
