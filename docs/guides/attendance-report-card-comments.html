Attendance report card comments: what to write about absences and lateness, with examples and a free builder

Attendance is the report card comment most likely to start an argument. Write "poor attendance" or "is always late" and a family hears blame, often for things a seven-year-old doesn't control; write nothing and a real problem goes unmentioned until it's much harder to fix. A good attendance comment states the pattern without judging, says what it means for learning, explains how school is helping, and invites the family in. Below: wording for good attendance, improvement, missed days, a block away, lateness, part days and missed lessons, 18 rewrites of phrases to avoid, six complete comments, and a free builder that writes and checks each comment in your character limit.

Updated 31 October 2026 · Grades K–12, with UK wording for Reception to Year 13 · About a 7-minute read

The short version: lead with a strength ("works hard in lessons"), then describe the pattern without blame ("has missed a number of school days this term"), what it means for learning ("which means missing new teaching that later lessons build on"), how school is helping ("we are sharing missed work and offering short catch-up sessions") and a shared goal ("our goal for next term is for Maya to be in school every day"). Close by inviting the family in. Leave out reasons and health details, family circumstances, numbers and percentages (the attendance record does the counting), fines and truancy warnings, and verdicts like "poor attendance" or "unacceptable". For good attendance, say what it makes possible. If attendance is a real concern, talk to the family first: the report shouldn't be the first they hear of it. Before you write: seven rules for attendance comments

  • Talk first, write second. If attendance is a real concern, call or meet the family before reports go home. Your school will already be in touch through its attendance process; the report comment should never be the first they hear of it.
  • Let the record do the counting. Most report cards print days absent and tardies, and in England the annual report must include the pupil's attendance record. Numbers in the comment go out of date and invite an argument about one day. Describe the pattern instead.
  • Leave reasons out. Illness, appointments, anxiety, a family holiday, a parent's shifts: even when the family has told you, the written report is the wrong place. It's also not your call in the comment whether an absence was "excused" or "authorised".
  • No warnings on a report. Fines, penalty notices, truancy, court and attendance officers belong in your school's attendance letters and meetings. On a report card they read as a threat and close the conversation you need to have.
  • Younger children don't run the school run. A six-year-old doesn't decide when they leave the house. "Has often arrived after the start of the school day" describes it without blaming the child, and "please let me know if anything is making mornings difficult" offers help to the adults. Secondary students can take more ownership: "has often arrived late for first period".
  • Describe, don't judge. "Poor attendance", "unacceptable", "lazy", "skips lessons" and "always late" are verdicts. "Has missed some lessons during the school day this term, which makes it harder to keep up with the course" says the same thing and gives a way forward.
  • Notice good attendance too. A specific line ("arrives on time every morning and settles quickly into the start of the day") and a thank-you to the family matter, especially for a child whose attendance has improved.

When attendance is a serious concern

In the US, missing 10% or more of school days for any reason, excused or not, is usually called chronic absence (Attendance Works). In a 180-day school year, 10% is 18 days: about two a month. In England, the Department for Education's statutory guidance calls missing 10% or more of sessions persistent absence (about a day a fortnight) and 50% or more severe absence, and expects schools to put support first, "rather than reaching immediately for punitive approaches" (Working together to improve school attendance, updated July 2026).

Those labels are for your school's attendance process, not the report. If a student is at or near those levels, your attendance lead, counselor or (in England) senior attendance champion and designated safeguarding lead should already be involved; follow your school's policy. Talk to the family before the report goes home. The comment can stay short and kind: a strength, the pattern, "please get in touch so we can meet and talk about how to support Maya together", and nothing about fines or legal steps.

UK reports: the attendance record and the comment

In England, a pupil's annual report must include their attendance record, alongside brief particulars of achievements and how to arrange a discussion about the report (DfE guidance). Your school's system usually prints the record for you, so the comment doesn't need to repeat the figure. Switch the builder to UK for "term", "lessons", "registration" and UK spelling. If your school uses attendance bands or colours on reports, keep them out of the comment too: a family seeing "red" next to their child's name is unlikely to read the rest kindly.

Common questions

What should I write on a report card about attendance?

State the pattern without blame, say what it means for learning, how school is helping, and a shared goal: "Leo has often arrived after the start of the school day this quarter, which means missing the start of our morning lessons. We give Leo a quick recap on arrival. Our goal for next quarter is for Leo to arrive in time for the start of the day." Lead with a strength, leave out reasons and numbers, and talk to the family before the report goes home if attendance is a real concern.

What are some positive attendance comments for report cards?

Be specific about what good attendance makes possible: "Isla has been in school every day this term and arrives ready to learn", "Maya arrives on time every morning and settles quickly into the start of the day", or for an improvement, "Jayden has been in school much more often this quarter, and it is already making a difference to his learning." A short thank-you to the family is a nice close.

How do I write about poor attendance without upsetting parents?

Avoid verdicts like "poor attendance" or "unacceptable". Describe what happened ("has missed a number of school days this term"), what it means for learning ("missing new teaching that later lessons build on"), what school is doing ("sharing missed work and offering short catch-up sessions") and invite them in ("please get in touch so we can meet and talk about how to support Maya together"). Never mention fines or legal steps in the report, and call the family first.

Should I put the number of absences or the attendance percentage in the comment?

Usually not. Most report cards print the attendance record already, and in England the annual report must include it. A number in the comment can be out of date by the time the report goes home, and invites an argument about one day. If your school wants it in the comment, check it against the register on the day the report is sent.

What do I write about a student who is often late (tardy)?

For younger children, remember they don't control the school run: "Leo has often arrived after the start of the school day this quarter, which means missing the start of our morning lessons." Add how you help ("we give Leo a warm welcome and a quick recap on arrival") and "if anything is making mornings difficult, please let me know so we can help." For secondary students, "has often arrived late for first period" is fine.

Should I mention why a student was absent, such as illness or a family holiday?

No. Keep reasons, health details and family circumstances out of the written report, even if the family told you. The attendance record already shows how each absence was recorded. Describe the effect on learning and how you are helping the student catch up.
