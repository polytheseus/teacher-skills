Report card comments for English language learners (ELL, ESL and EAL): examples by stage, and a free comment builder

A student who arrived in September with almost no English has still learned a lot by November: the class routines, the names of their friends, how to ask for the bathroom, the words for this month's science topic. The report card should say so. Here's how to write a comment that tells the family what their child can do in English now, keeps what they know in math or science separate from their English, and is plain enough to survive a translation app. Below: wording for five stages of English across listening, speaking, reading and writing, 18 rewrites of phrases to avoid, and a free builder that writes and checks each comment.

Updated 30 October 2026 · Grades 1–8, with UK (EAL) wording · About an 8-minute read

The short version: a good comment for an English learner does six things. A real strength first ("has a go at speaking English, even when unsure"). What they show in other subjects, so English isn't the whole picture ("shows a good understanding of math with pictures and models"). What they can do in English now, in one of the four areas: listening, speaking, reading, writing. One next step. The support in class. A home tip, and the best one is usually "keep reading and talking together in your home language". Then cut the idioms and long sentences, because many families will read it in their second language or through a translation app. The builder below puts it together and flags the wording to avoid.

Before you write: four things that are different for English learners

  • Subject knowledge and English are two different things. A student can understand fractions perfectly and still be unable to explain them in an English sentence. Follow your district's grading policy, but where you can, grade what the student knows (shown with models, drawings, a word bank or a home-language explanation if your policy allows) and report their English separately. The comment can then say both.
  • The four areas grow at different speeds. WIDA's guide to its ACCESS score reports (Spring 2026) notes that speaking and listening often develop faster than reading and writing, that listening and reading often develop faster than speaking and writing, and that it's not unusual for writing to take longest. So pick the strongest area for "can do now" and the next area for "next step"; it's normal for them to differ.
  • The home language is an asset. The same WIDA guide says a student's foundation in their home language is a good predictor of English language development. Never ask families to switch to English at home; ask them to keep reading and talking in the language they speak best.
  • Write for translation. Many families will read the comment in their second or third language, or paste it into a translation app. Short sentences (about 20 words or fewer), no idioms ("on track", "leaps and bounds", "coming out of her shell") and no teacher jargon ("decoding", "scaffolds", "receptive language") make that work. If your school translates reports, WIDA's advice for its own score reports applies here too: a translated copy should go with the official English one, not replace it.

Strengths to start with

For a student in their first months, the strength doesn't have to be academic. Families often worry most about whether their child is happy and has friends, so a true line about that is the one they'll remember.

  • Mateo has a go at speaking English, even when unsure, which takes real courage.
  • Aisha has settled well into our class routines.
  • Wei has made good friends and joins in at recess.
  • Sofia is a kind classmate who notices when others need help.
  • Yusuf works hard in every lesson and takes pride in his work.
  • Hana asks questions when something is new, which helps her learning.
  • Dmitri listens carefully and watches closely to understand what's happening.
  • Lina has taught our class words and greetings in her home language.
  • Kenji is quick to help in class, whether that's handing out books or tidying up.
  • Amira comes into class with a positive attitude and a smile.

And what they show in other subjects:

  • Aisha shows a good understanding of math, especially when problems use numbers, pictures and models.
  • Wei understands our science topics well and shows it in diagrams, models and practical work.
  • Sofia expresses ideas confidently in art and design.
  • Yusuf joins in fully in PE and games, which is also a great way to build everyday English.
  • Hana brings real enthusiasm to music and picks up songs quickly.
  • Dmitri reads and writes in his home language, which is a real strength to build on.
  • Lina shows understanding in many ways, including drawings, models and talking with a partner.

Home tips that work in any language

  • At home, keep reading, talking and telling stories together in your home language. A strong home language helps children learn English.
  • At home, ask Sofia to tell you about the school day, in any language. Talking about learning helps it stick.
  • At home, borrow library books in English and in your home language, and talk about the pictures together.
  • At home, listen to stories or audiobooks in English together for a few minutes a day. Hearing English for fun builds vocabulary.
  • At home, ask Dmitri to teach you three new English words from school each week.

Common questions

How do you write a report card comment for an English language learner?

Start with a real strength, then say what the student can do in English now (listening, speaking, reading or writing), name one next step, say what support is in place, and give the family one home tip, ideally reading and talking in the home language. Keep what the student knows in the subject separate from their English, and keep sentences short so the comment translates well.

Should report card grades for ELLs reflect their English?

Follow your district's grading policy. A common approach is to grade what the student knows and can do in the subject, using visuals, models, word banks or a home-language explanation where your policy allows, and to report English language development separately. The comment can then say both: what the student understands in the subject, and what's next in English.

Should I tell parents to speak English at home?

No. WIDA's score report guide notes that a student's foundation in their home language is a good predictor of English language development. Encourage families to keep reading, talking and telling stories in the language they speak best.

Can I put a student's WIDA or ACCESS level in a report card comment?

A level number means little to most families. Describe what the student can do instead. If your school reports the English proficiency level, it usually has its own box or a separate score report, which WIDA offers in translated versions to go with the English one.

What should I leave out of a comment for an ELL or EAL student?

Immigration or legal status, where the family is from, what languages the parents do or don't speak, family circumstances, comparisons with native speakers or other learners, and any guess at a learning disability. Learning a new language is not a learning difficulty; if you have a concern, raise it with your EL and special education colleagues and the family.

Can I use ChatGPT to write ELL report card comments?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names, home countries, immigration details and family information. Give it a code for each student, their stage of English and your notes, ask for short plain sentences without idioms, and read every comment before it goes home.
