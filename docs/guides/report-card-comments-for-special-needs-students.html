Report card comments for special needs students: 146 examples, IEP progress notes and a free builder

A report card comment for a student with an IEP, a 504 plan or special educational needs is the one families read most closely. They're looking for what their child can do now, whether anything is working, and what comes next. They don't need the diagnosis or the plan repeated back to them. Here's a pattern that does that, with wording for 12 strengths and 15 skills students work on (what they do now, what's helping, the next step and a home idea), 40 short comments, 22 rewrites of phrases to avoid, a separate section on IEP progress report notes, and a free builder that writes the comment and checks it. Want them on paper? Download every line as a printable PDF (free).

Updated 10 November 2026 · Grades K–8, with UK wording · About a 9-minute read

The short version: a good comment for a student with special needs does four things. Starts with something going well ("keeps trying when work is hard and is proud of each step forward"). Says what the student is working on now, as something they do ("is working on reading longer words by breaking them into parts"). Says what's helping ("daily practice in a small group is helping"). Names the next step ("Next quarter, the goal is for Leo to read new words with fewer stops"). Leave out diagnoses and labels, the IEP or 504 plan itself (unless your school says otherwise), therapy and staffing details, comparisons with classmates, "can't" and "won't", and backhanded praise like "despite his disability". Progress on IEP goals goes in the IEP progress report, which is a different document. The builder below puts the comment together and flags the wording to avoid.

The four-part pattern

PartWhat it doesExample
1. Something going wellShows you see the whole child, not the need"Leo keeps trying when work is hard and is proud of each step forward."
2. What they're working on nowDescribes a skill, not a deficit"He is working on reading longer words by breaking them into parts."
3. What's helpingTells the family what works, in plain words"Daily practice in a small group, with books matched to his reading, is helping."
4. The next stepSmall and specific, so progress next time is visible"Next quarter, the goal is for Leo to read new words in a story with fewer stops to sound them out."

Add a line on progress since the start of the period whenever you can ("Since the start of the quarter, Leo has learned all of the letter sounds"). For a student whose progress is slow, that sentence is often the one the family remembers.

Before you write: five things that are different

  • The family already knows the diagnosis. Repeating it ("Leo's ADHD makes it hard for him to focus") adds nothing for them, and report cards get forwarded, photographed and kept for years. Describe what you see and what helps instead.
  • The report card and the IEP progress report are different documents. Under the US special education regulations, each IEP has to say how progress toward its annual goals will be measured and when periodic progress reports will be given, "such as through the use of quarterly or other periodic reports, concurrent with the issuance of report cards" (34 CFR 300.320(a)(3)). Goal data belongs there. The report card comment is about the student's learning in your class. More on IEP progress notes below.
  • Follow your school's policy on naming plans and services. Many schools keep IEPs, 504 plans, therapy and support staff out of the general report card. If yours expects you to mention accommodations or a modified curriculum, it will usually say exactly how. If you're not sure, ask your special education team before the reports go home.
  • Grade-level gaps are already on the report. If the student is working well below grade level, the grade or level box usually shows it. The comment's job is what they can do now and what comes next. Small steps count.
  • Talk first about anything hard. If there's a concern the family hasn't heard yet, a phone call or meeting comes before the report card, not after it.

IEP progress report notes: a different job

The IEP progress report goes home on the schedule written in the IEP, often alongside report cards. It answers one question for each annual goal: is the student on track to meet it? The report card comment can stay general. The progress note has to be specific and measured, in the way the IEP says the goal will be measured.

PartWhat to write
The goalAs written in the IEP. Don't reword it.
The dataWhat you measured, when, and the result, with the starting point (baseline) for comparison.
The progress ratingYour district's code or wording (for example "making progress", "not yet making expected progress", "goal met"). Use theirs exactly.
What happens nextOne sentence: continue, adjust the support, or (if progress has stalled) suggest the team meets.

Four example notes (the goals and numbers are invented, to show the shape):

Goal: By the annual review, given a passage at the second-grade level, Leo will read 60 words correctly per minute in 3 of 4 trials.
Note: Baseline in September: 35 words correctly per minute. In three checks in October, Leo read 41, 44 and 47 words correctly per minute. Making progress; on track to meet the goal if this rate continues. Next: continue daily small-group fluency practice and add partner reading twice a week.

Goal: By the annual review, Maya will move between classroom activities with no more than one adult prompt in 4 of 5 opportunities.
Note: Baseline: 1 of 5 opportunities. Over two weeks of tallies in October, Maya needed one prompt or none in 3 of 5 transitions on average, using her visual schedule. Making progress. Next: start fading the verbal warning before each change, keeping the schedule.

Goal: By the annual review, given a one-step addition or subtraction word problem within 20, Amara will choose the correct operation and solve it with 80% accuracy.
Note: Baseline: 30%. On two probes in October Amara scored 40% and 45%; she chooses the right operation more often when she draws the problem first. Making some progress, below the rate needed to meet the goal. Next: teach drawing the problem as a routine first step; review again in December.

Goal: By the annual review, Noah will use a calming strategy from his plan, with one adult prompt, when upset in 4 of 5 recorded episodes.
Note: Baseline: 0 of 5. In October, Noah used a strategy with one prompt in 2 of 6 recorded episodes. Making progress. Next: practise the strategies daily at a calm time, and check the plan with Noah and his family.

Write progress notes in the words your district's IEP system uses, and keep the data your team agreed to collect. If a student isn't making the progress needed to meet a goal, raise it with the IEP team rather than waiting for the annual review.

Common questions

How do you write a report card comment for a student with special needs?

Start with something going well, then say what the student is working on now as a skill they are learning, what is helping, and one next step. For example: "Leo keeps trying when work is hard. He is working on reading longer words by breaking them into parts, and daily practice in a small group is helping. Next quarter, the goal is for Leo to read new words in a story with fewer stops." Leave out diagnoses, labels and comparisons with classmates.

Should I mention the IEP, 504 plan or diagnosis on the report card?

Follow your school's policy. Many schools keep diagnoses, plans, therapy and support staff out of the general report card comment, and report on IEP goals in the IEP progress report instead. The family already knows the diagnosis, and report cards get shared and kept. Describe what the student does and what helps in plain words.

What is the difference between a report card comment and an IEP progress report?

The report card comment is about the student's learning in your class. The IEP progress report answers, for each annual goal, whether the student is on track to meet it, using the measurement written in the IEP. US special education rules require each IEP to say how goal progress is measured and when periodic reports are given, often alongside report cards (34 CFR 300.320(a)(3)).

What are some positive report card comments for special needs students?

Specific ones about something the student really does: "keeps trying when work is hard and is proud of each step forward", "communicates their needs clearly, using words, signs or pictures", "is a kind friend who notices when others need help", "has grown in confidence as a reader this quarter". Then add what they are working on next, so the comment is honest as well as kind.

What are some short report card comments for students with special needs?

Short ones that still say something: "Keeps trying when work is hard and is proud of each step." "Starts tasks independently when the steps are broken down." "Uses a calming strategy when upset, with a reminder." "Next, working on sharing an idea with the whole class." This page has 40 of them, all under 100 characters, in the list and in the free PDF.

Only within your school's AI policy, and only without names, diagnoses, plans, therapy or family details. Give it a code for each student and your notes on what is going well, what they are working on, what helps and the next step. Then read every comment against what you know of the student.
