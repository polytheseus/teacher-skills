Kindergarten report card comments: 90 examples by skill, and a free comment builder

Kindergarten comments are read by families who have never had a report card before, about a child who was in preschool a few months ago. They need to be warm, specific and honest, with no jargon and no verdicts. Here are comments for every common kindergarten skill at three levels, kind wording for the hard ones, a home tip for each skill, and a free builder that writes a comment from your choices and checks it.

Updated 28 October 2026 · US kindergarten, with notes for UK Reception and Australian Foundation · About an 8-minute read

The short version: each comment does four things. One or two specific strengths ("knows all 26 letter names"), one next step and how you're working on it ("our next step is counting objects one at a time, with counting games"), one thing the family can do at home ("count stairs or snacks together") and a warm close. Write about this child's progress only, use plain words instead of standards codes, and remember that kindergarten standards describe the end of the year: in November, "still learning" is normal. The comment builder below puts it together and flags the words to avoid.

The kindergarten comment formula

PartWhat it doesExample
1. Opening or momentShows you know the child"I loved seeing Maya count every block in the tower she built."
2. StrengthsOne or two skills, named specifically"In reading, Maya knows the names of all 26 letters, capital and lowercase."
3. Next stepOne skill, framed as what comes next, plus what school is doing"Our next step is writing her name, which we're practicing in our daily writing time."
4. Home tipOne small, concrete activity"At home, you could let Maya write shopping lists or cards, however the spelling comes out."
5. CloseWarm, forward-looking"I'm looking forward to seeing Maya's progress next quarter."

Two or three skills is plenty. The report card's grade boxes already cover everything else; the comment is where a parent finds out what to do next.

First quarter? Kindergarten standards describe June, not November

The skills on most US kindergarten report cards come from end-of-year standards. In the Common Core, for example, kindergartners should by the end of the year be able to count to 100 by ones and by tens (K.CC.1), write numbers from 0 to 20 (K.CC.3), fluently add and subtract within 5 (K.OA.5), recognize and name all upper- and lowercase letters (RF.K.1d) and read common high-frequency words by sight (RF.K.3c). Math and reading foundations standards are on the Common Core site; your state's may differ, so check yours.

So a child who knows 15 letters in November isn't "below grade level". They're partway through the year. Comments in the first and second quarter work best when they say what the child can do now, what's next, and, where it's true, "which is right on track for this point in the year". Save "not yet meeting" for the grade boxes, and only once your school's benchmarks say so.

Needs-improvement comments, written kindly

The hardest comments are the ones where a child is finding something difficult. Parents of a five-year-old read them very closely. Say what the child is learning, not what they're failing at, and always pair it with what you're doing and what helps.

Instead ofWrite
Maya can't write her name.Maya is learning to write her name and can already write the M. We practice name writing every morning.
Leo is behind in reading.Leo knows 12 letter names and is adding new ones each week. Our next step is the rest of the alphabet, starting with the letters in his name.
Sam is immature and cries a lot.Sam is learning to name their feelings and to ask a grown-up for help when upset. We use our calm corner and feelings chart every day.
Ava never listens at carpet time.Ava is working on listening at group time and waiting for her turn to talk. A short, clear signal and sitting near the front help her.
Noah is very disruptive.Noah has lots of energy and great ideas. Our next step is keeping his hands to himself at carpet time, and movement breaks between activities are helping.
Zara refuses to share.Zara is learning to take turns and share in play. We practice with turn-taking games, and she is starting to offer toys to friends.
Eli is lazy about tidying up.Eli is becoming more independent at tidy-up time and does best with one job at a time ("put the blocks in the blue box").
Ruby's counting is weak compared to the class.Ruby counts to 10 confidently and is learning to touch each object once as she counts. Counting snacks or stairs at home helps a lot.
Omar may have a speech delay.Don't write it. Talk to the family in person and follow your school's process (speech-language screening, the student support team). The report can say: "Omar is growing in confidence when speaking in a group."

UK Reception reports

In England, the end-of-Reception assessment is the EYFS profile. Each child is assessed against the 17 early learning goals across the 7 areas of learning, and for each goal is judged as "expected" or "emerging" (not yet reaching the expected level). The profile is completed at the end of the year (local authorities must have it by 30 June), and the government's EYFS profile handbook says it isn't meant for ongoing assessment during the year.

So in an autumn or spring Reception report, avoid "expected" and "emerging": they're end-of-year judgements, and a parent who reads "emerging" in November may hear "failing". Describe progress, the next step and a home tip, exactly as above. Switch the builder to UK Reception for Reception, maths, term, Year 1 and UK spelling. Your school's report format and the EYFS profile results it shares with families in the summer term come first.

Australian Foundation, Prep and Kindergarten reports

In Australia the first year of school is Kindergarten (NSW, ACT), Prep (Vic, Qld, Tas), Reception (SA), Pre-primary (WA), Transition (NT) or Foundation (the Australian Curriculum), and the school year ends in December, so the end-of-year report is the Semester 2 one. Common questions

What should I write in a kindergarten report card comment?

One or two specific strengths, one skill the child is working on next and how you're practising it, one small home activity, and a warm close. Keep it to the child's own progress, in plain words, and mention a real moment if you can.

How do I write a comment for a kindergartener who is struggling?

Start with a real strength, then describe the skill as a next step: "is still learning to" or "is working on" instead of "can't" or "is behind". Say what you're doing in class and give one home activity. If you're seriously worried, talk to the family in person first, and never suggest a diagnosis in writing.

Is it normal not to know all the letters in the first quarter?

Yes. Kindergarten standards such as the Common Core describe the end of the year, for example naming all upper- and lowercase letters and counting to 100 by ones and tens. In the first quarter, report what the child can do now and what comes next.

How long should a kindergarten comment be?

Follow your school's limit; many report systems allow somewhere between 400 and 1,000 characters. Three to six short sentences is usually enough.

Can I use ChatGPT to write kindergarten report card comments?

Within your school's AI policy, with codes instead of names and no sensitive details. Give it your skill notes, ask for a fixed pattern (strength, next step, home tip), then read every comment and add the names back.
