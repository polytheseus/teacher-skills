Report card comments for behavior and work habits: 128 examples, Ontario learning skills, and a free comment builder

"Ethan is disruptive" tells a family something is wrong, but not what it is or what to do about it. This guide gives you behavior and work-habit comments for grades K–8: listening, staying on task, working independently, organization, homework, effort, kindness, teamwork, managing feelings, routines, waiting for a turn and more. Each habit has strengths, on-track lines, a next step and a home tip. There are also 22 rewrites of phrases to avoid, a section on Ontario's six learning skills and work habits, six complete comments, and a free builder that writes and checks each one.

The short version: describe the habit, not the child. Open with a real strength ("notices when others need help and offers it without being asked"), say what's on track, give one next step with what class is doing about it ("waiting for his turn to speak, which we're practicing with talk partners"), and end with one small routine to try at home. Leave out labels ("disruptive", "lazy", "bossy"), verdicts ("bad attitude"), incidents and sanctions (detentions, clip charts, trips to the office), diagnoses, and "always" or "never". If behavior is a real concern, the family should hear it from you before they read it. The builder below puts the comment together and checks the wording.

A moment you noticed beats any stock phrase: "I was impressed when Leo noticed a new classmate on his own at recess and invited him to join the game" tells the family you know their child. Many report cards rate work habits and conduct with codes or tick boxes; the comment is where you explain the one or two that matter, so pick two or three habits, not all of them.

When behavior is a real concern

Most behavior comments are easy. The hard ones are for the student whose behavior has been a problem all term. The report card is the wrong place for a family to find that out, and the wrong place for the detail. A few rules most experienced teachers follow:

  • Talk first. If a family will be surprised by the comment, call or meet them before the report goes home. The comment should confirm a conversation, not start one.
  • Describe what happens, not who the child is. "Sometimes calls out during discussions" is something a family can help with; "disruptive" is a label they'll remember for years, and so will the child, who may read it.
  • Say when, not "always". "After lunch", "in group work", "when a task feels hard" are more accurate and point to the fix.
  • Say what's in place. A seating change, a signal, a calm-down space, short goals with a check-in. Families want to know the school has a plan.
  • One next step. Pick the behavior that would make the biggest difference. A list of five problems gives the family nothing to act on.
  • Leave out incidents, sanctions and diagnoses. Detentions, office referrals, clip charts, suspensions, ADHD, medication and support plans belong in conversations and in your school's records, under its policy.
  • Still start with a strength. Every student has one. If you can't name one, that's worth noticing before you write anything else.

Ontario learning skills and work habits comments

In Ontario, report cards for Grades 1 to 12 rate six learning skills and work habits separately from subject marks: Responsibility, Organization, Independent Work, Collaboration, Initiative and Self-Regulation. Each is rated E (Excellent), G (Good), S (Satisfactory) or N (Needs Improvement). In Grades 1 to 8 the homeroom teacher usually completes this section, and the policy (Growing Success, 2010) asks the comment to cover the student's "strengths" and "next steps for improvement". It also says that, as far as possible, these skills should not count towards a subject's letter grade or percentage mark.

The habits in this guide map onto the six skills like this (the policy's sample behaviors include completing work on time, following instructions with minimal supervision, responding positively to others' ideas, showing curiosity, and persevering when work is challenging):

Middle school: separate the work from the habits

  • Keep habits out of the grade where you can. If a low grade is mostly down to missing homework, say so in the comment, and say what fixes it under your actual late-work policy. Families can't help with "needs to apply himself".
  • Write so the student can act on it. From about grade 6, students read their own report cards. "Using a planner to keep track of homework and deadlines" is something a 12-year-old can do this week.
  • Name the help that exists. Homework club, the class page with deadlines, a weekly planner check, office hours. Families often don't know about them.
  • One subject teacher, one habit. If several teachers write about the same student, agree who covers behavior, so the family doesn't read the same concern six times.

Home tips that families actually use

The best behavior home tips are routines, not punishments: a five-minute warning before changing activities, packing the school bag the night before, a regular homework time and place, taking turns to talk at mealtimes, one family job that's always yours, naming feelings out loud when things are calm. One tip per comment, tied to the next step, is enough. Avoid tips that ask the family to punish at home for what happened at school; that turns the report into a second sanction.

No labels (disruptive, lazy, defiant, bossy, talkative), no verdicts (bad attitude), no incidents or sanctions, no diagnoses, no "always" or "never", no comparisons with other students or siblings, no jargon (executive function, off-task, PBIS). Don't use "a pleasure to have in class", "good as gold" or "journey". Use [Name] and they/them; I'll add names and pronouns.

S1: strength kind, includes others; on track homework; next step waiting for turn to speak (talk partners, signal)
S2: strength great questions; next step staying on task in independent work (short goals, check-in)
S3: strength perseveres; next step organizing materials (end-of-day tidy routine)

Then read every comment, check it really fits that student, and paste any you're unsure of into the checker above.

Common questions

What should a behavior report card comment include?

One specific strength (a habit the student does well, such as including others or listening to the whole instruction), whether the rest is on track, one next step framed as something the student is learning to do, with what class is doing about it, and one small routine to try at home. Describe the behavior, not the child.

How do I write a report card comment about bad behavior?

Talk to the family first, so the comment confirms a conversation rather than starting one. Start with a real strength, then describe what happens and when ("sometimes calls out during discussions"), the support in place ("a signal for when it's time to share") and one next step. Leave out labels such as disruptive or defiant, incidents and sanctions, diagnoses, and "always" or "never".

What are work habits on a report card?

The habits that help a student learn, separate from subject grades: listening and following directions, staying on task, working independently, organization, completing work on time, effort and taking care with work. Many report cards rate them with codes or tick boxes and leave room for a comment on one or two.

What are the six learning skills in Ontario?

Responsibility, Organization, Independent Work, Collaboration, Initiative and Self-Regulation. Ontario report cards for Grades 1 to 12 rate each one E (Excellent), G (Good), S (Satisfactory) or N (Needs Improvement), separately from subject marks, and the comment covers the student's strengths and next steps for improvement (Growing Success, 2010).

Can I use ChatGPT to write behavior report card comments?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names, incidents, sanctions, diagnoses, support plans and family details. Give it short notes per student with a code instead of a name, ask for the strength, next step and home tip pattern with no labels, and read every comment before it goes on a report.
