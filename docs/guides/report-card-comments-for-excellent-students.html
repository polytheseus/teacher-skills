Report card comments for excellent students: 144 specific examples, 50 short comments and a free builder

The students who do everything right often get the thinnest comments: "A pleasure to have in class. Keep up the good work." Their families read those lines looking for something, and there's nothing there. Here's a pattern that says what a high achiever really does and what comes next, with comments in 14 areas, 14 next challenges, 50 short comments for tight boxes, 24 rewrites of stock phrases, and a free builder that writes the comment and checks it. Updated 9 November 2026 · Grades 1–8, with UK wording · About an 8-minute read

The short version: a comment for an excellent student does four things. Says what they actually do ("backs up her ideas about a text with evidence from the page"). Gives one real moment from this report period. Names the next challenge, something harder in kind and not just more of the same. Says who'll do what about it. Leave out what could be about anyone ("a pleasure to have in class"), what they are ("gifted", "bright"), and how they compare with classmates ("top of the class"). The grade already says the work is excellent. The comment's job is the why and the what next. The builder below puts it together and flags the stock phrases.

The four-part pattern

PartWhat it doesExample
1. What they doSpecific enough that it couldn't be about another student"Leo spots patterns and uses them to solve new kinds of problems."
2. One real momentProves you noticed; the line families read aloud"One highlight this quarter: Leo solved the bridge problem three different ways."
3. The next challengeAnswers the question these families are really asking"Next quarter, he is ready to explain why a method works as well as how to use it."
4. Who does whatShows the challenge is real, with one thing for you and one for home"To help with this, I'll add problems with more than one route. At home, you could ask Leo to explain a tricky problem as if teaching it to you."

If you only have room for two parts, keep the first and the third. Keep the moment whenever you can, though: it's the part that shows you know the child.

Five ways these comments go wrong

  • Praise that fits anyone. "Excellent student" is true of several children in the room. If you could paste the sentence into another student's report without changing a word, it isn't doing anything.
  • Praising what they are. "Bright", "gifted", "a natural". In a well-known 1998 paper by Mueller and Dweck, children praised for intelligence after a success did worse and enjoyed the work less after a later failure than children praised for effort. That was a lab study and one line on a report won't change a child, but it's a good reason to write about the work, which also gives the family something to talk about.
  • Comparing. "Top of the class" and "far ahead of the others" tell one family how another family's child is doing. Report cards get shared. Keep it to this student's own work.
  • No next step. "There is nothing to improve" sounds like the highest praise, but it says there's nothing left to teach them. Families of strong students usually want to know what comes next.
  • The student as classroom assistant. Being kind and explaining things well is a real strength. "Helps the weaker students finish their work" calls classmates weak in writing and suggests the student's time goes on other people's learning. Name the kindness, and give them a challenge of their own as well.

Middle school: write to the student too

From about grade 6, students read their own comments, and a strong student will look for the part that's meant for them.

  • Name what's actually good about the work. "Your argument about the causes of the war used three sources well" is worth more to a 13-year-old than any adjective.
  • Make the challenge something they could do next week. "Try a counter-argument in your next essay" beats "continue to challenge yourself".
  • Stick to your subject. If you teach one class, write about that class; the advisor or form tutor sees the whole picture.

Common questions

What do you write on a report card for an excellent student?

Say what the student actually does well, in words that couldn't be about anyone else, then name the next challenge. For example: "Maya backs up her ideas about a text with evidence from the page. Next quarter, she is ready to read more challenging texts and compare how different authors handle the same idea." The grade already says the work is excellent; the comment says why, and what comes next.

What are some short report card comments for excellent students?

Short ones that still say something: "Explains their thinking clearly and listens well to others." "Sticks with hard problems and tries new approaches." "Uses feedback well and each piece of work improves." "Ready to lead a group project next quarter." This page has 50 of them, all under 100 characters, in the list and in the free PDF.

Should I write "gifted" or "bright" on a report card?

Usually not. Those words describe what a student is, not what they did, and families can't do much with them. Write what the student does: "spots patterns and uses them to solve new kinds of problems". If a student is in a formal gifted program, follow your school's rule on naming it. Otherwise the comment doesn't need the word.

What is a good next step for a student who already gets top grades?

Something harder in kind, not more of the same. Reasons as well as answers, a task that can't be done right the first time, a question of their own to research, a group to lead, or sharing ideas more often if they're quiet. Avoid "more worksheets" and avoid making the student the class helper.

What should I write for a good student who isn't top of the class?

Use the same pattern. Name what they do well ("plans longer projects in stages and meets deadlines"), then one specific next step ("use feedback to make strong work even better"). A steady student is easy to overlook when you're writing 30 comments, so a specific line tends to mean a lot.

Can I use ChatGPT to write comments for high-achieving students?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names and private details. Give it a code for each student, your notes on what they did, and the next step, and ask it to avoid stock phrases. Then read every comment: generic praise is exactly what these tools produce when the notes are thin.
