First grade report card comments: 108 examples by skill, and a free comment builder

First grade is where parents start asking "is my child reading yet?", so a vague "doing well" doesn't help them. Here are comments for 18 first grade skills at three levels, from sounding out words to tens and ones, plus short general comments, kind needs-improvement wording, what to say when a child really isn't on track, and a free builder that writes and checks each comment.

Updated 2 November 2026 · US first grade, with notes for Canada (Grade 1), UK and Australian Year 1 · About an 8-minute read

The short version: each comment does four things. One or two specific strengths ("sounds out new words, including words with sh, ch and th"), one next step and how you're working on it ("our next step is writing two or three sentences on one idea, in our daily writing time"), one thing the family can do at home ("write a note or a list together") and a warm close. Write about this child's progress only, in plain words, with no reading-level codes. Jump to the reading, writing, math or work habits comments, the short general comments, or the free builder.

The first grade comment formula

PartWhat it doesExample
1. Opening or momentShows you know the child"I loved seeing Maya read a whole page to the class in a dinosaur voice."
2. StrengthsOne or two skills, named specifically"In reading, Maya sounds out new words confidently, including words with sh, ch and th."
3. Next stepOne skill, framed as what comes next, plus what school is doing"Our next step is writing two or three sentences on one idea, which we're practicing in our daily writing time."
4. Home tipOne small, concrete activity"At home, you could write something real together, like a note, a card or a list."
5. CloseWarm, forward-looking"I'm looking forward to seeing Maya's progress next quarter."

Two or three skills is plenty. The grade boxes cover the rest; the comment is where a parent finds out what to do next.

First quarter? First grade standards describe June, not November

Most first grade report card skills come from end-of-year standards. In the Common Core, for example, first graders should by the end of the year be able to count to 120 from any number below it (1.NBT.A.1), add and subtract within 20, and be fluent within 10 (1.OA.C.6), understand two-digit numbers as tens and ones (1.NBT.B.2), tell time to the hour and half hour (1.MD.B.3), and know the silent e and common vowel teams (RF.1.3.c). Not every state uses the Common Core, so check yours.

So a child who is adding within 10 with counters in November isn't "below grade level" for that reason alone. They're partway through the year. Early in the year, say what the child can do now, what's next and, where it's true, "which is right on track for this point in the year". That line has to be true, though: if your school's own checks say a child needs extra help, read what to say when a child really isn't on track.

When a child really isn't on track

Kind wording mustn't hide a real concern. If a first grader is well below where your school expects, and a family reads "making progress" for two quarters, the report has let them down. The sooner a family knows, the sooner they can help. So:

  • Talk first. A call or a meeting should come before the report, so the report isn't how they find out.
  • Be concrete. Say what the child reads or does now, and what you're aiming for next, in words a parent can picture.
  • Name the help. Say what's actually happening, such as small-group reading four mornings a week. Don't promise anything your school hasn't agreed.
  • Invite a conversation. Offer a time to talk, rather than ending on a reassurance.
  • Don't diagnose. Words like dyslexia or ADHD belong to your school's evaluation process and the professionals in it, not a report comment.

Theo knows most of his letter sounds and is reading short words with help. He is still working on reading simple sentences on his own, and I'm giving him extra reading practice in a small group four mornings a week. At home, reading the same short book two or three times would really help. As we discussed, I'd like to meet again in January to look at his progress together.

Only write "four mornings a week" and "as we discussed" if they're true.

In England, Year 1 children take the phonics screening check in June: 40 words read aloud to a teacher, some of them made up. Common questions

What should I write in a first grade report card comment?

One or two specific strengths, one skill the child is working on next and how you're practicing it, one small home activity, and a warm close. Keep it to the child's own progress, in plain words, and mention a real moment if you can.

What can I write for a first grader who is struggling with reading?

Start with what they can read now, then say what comes next and what you're doing about it, such as small-group reading. Add one home activity, like rereading a short book. If the concern is serious, talk to the family before the report goes home, and never suggest a diagnosis in writing.

Should a first grade comment include a reading level?

Only if your school requires it, and then explain it. "Level E" or a Lexile number means little to most families. Say what the child reads: "simple books with a repeating pattern". Levels and expectations vary by school, so don't compare the child with other children.

How long should a first grade comment be?

Follow your school's limit; many report systems allow somewhere between 400 and 1,000 characters. Three to six short sentences is usually enough. If your limit is very small, use one of the short comments above.

Can I use ChatGPT to write first grade report card comments?

Within your school's AI policy, with codes instead of names and no sensitive details. Give it your skill notes, ask for a fixed pattern (strength, next step, home tip), then read every comment and add the names back.
