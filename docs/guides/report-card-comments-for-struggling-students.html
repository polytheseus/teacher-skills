Report card comments for struggling students: honest, kind examples, and a free comment builder

The hardest comments to write are for the student who is behind in reading, missing half their assignments, or finding it hard to sit still. Too soft and the family doesn't realise there's a problem until June. Too blunt and they stop reading after the first line. Here's a pattern that is honest and kind at the same time, comments for 15 common difficulties, 28 rewrites of the phrases teachers are told to avoid, and a free builder that writes the comment and checks it.

Updated 28 October 2026 · Grades 1–8, with UK wording · About a 9-minute read

The short version: a comment for a struggling student does five things. A real strength first ("keeps trying when work is difficult"). The difficulty, in words about what the student does ("often leaves classwork unfinished", not "lazy"). What school is already doing ("a weekly checklist we go through on Mondays"). One small thing for home. An invitation to talk. And one rule before you write: no surprises. If it's serious, the family should hear it from you by phone or in person before the report goes home. The builder below puts it together and flags the words to avoid.

The five-part pattern

PartWhat it doesExample
1. StrengthShows you see the whole child, and earns the next sentence"Leo asks great questions and is curious about how things work."
2. The difficultyHonest, specific, observable; no labels or guesses about feelings"Right now, Leo is still finding longer words tricky to read accurately."
3. The supportTells the family it isn't all on them or the child"To help, he reads with me in a small group four times a week."
4. Home tipOne small, concrete thing"At home, ten minutes of reading together most days, taking turns page by page, would help."
5. InvitationOpens a conversation instead of ending one"Please get in touch if you'd like to talk about how it's going."

If there's real progress since the last report, say so after the strength, with what changed ("now reads chapter books with a partner"). Families of a struggling student often only hear about the struggle; a specific improvement is the line they remember.

Before you write: three checks

  • No surprises. A report card shouldn't be the first time a family hears that their child is failing a subject, has missing work piling up, or is in trouble most days. Call, email or meet first; then the comment can match what you've already talked about ("Thank you for talking this through with me").
  • Is it the comment or the grade? The grade already says "below expectations". The comment's job is the why and the what next. If the grade is low because of missing work rather than understanding, say that, and say how to fix it (which assignments, by when).
  • What stays off the page. Unless your school's policy says otherwise, leave out diagnoses or guesses at one ("I think he has ADHD"), medication, IEP, 504, EHCP or other support-plan details, counselling, family circumstances and comparisons with other students. Report cards get forwarded, photographed and kept for years.

Positive comments for struggling students

Every student has something you can honestly start with. The strength doesn't have to be academic, but it does have to be specific and true. Generic praise ("a pleasure to have in class") before a hard sentence reads as a cushion, and families notice.

  • Maya is a kind classmate who notices when others need help.
  • Leo keeps trying when work is difficult, which is one of the most useful habits a learner can have.
  • Amara is getting better at asking for help when something doesn't make sense.
  • Ethan brings thoughtful ideas to our class discussions.
  • Sofia has a creative imagination that shows in her stories, drawings and projects.
  • Noah is quick to help, whether that's handing out books or showing a new student around.
  • Grace listens carefully to others and responds respectfully.
  • Omar asks great questions and is curious about how things work.
  • Lily comes alive in hands-on work, such as experiments, building and practical tasks.
  • Kai brings real energy and enthusiasm to PE.
  • Zoe explains her thinking clearly when talking it through.
  • Sam works well in a team and is a loyal friend.
  • Isla comes into class with a positive attitude and a smile.

If you're stuck, think about what the student does at break or lunch, in PE, art or music, how they treat classmates, or one thing that's better than in September.

Middle school: write to the student too

From about grade 6, students read their own report cards, and many families hand the comment straight to them. A few things change:

  • Be precise about missing work. "Three lab reports are missing; if they're handed in by 20 November, the grade will be recalculated" is something a 12-year-old can act on. "Needs to complete work" isn't. Use your school's actual late-work policy.
  • Name the habit, not the personality. "Often starts homework the night before it's due" rather than "disorganised".
  • Point to the help that exists. Homework club, office hours, a tutoring period, the class page. Families often don't know these exist.
  • Keep it to one subject's view. If you teach one subject, write about your class; the advisor or form tutor sees the whole picture.

UK reports

The same pattern works for a UK school report: switch the builder to UK for maths, practise and "term". Use your school's own attainment language (many primaries use "working towards", "at" or "greater depth" against the expected standard), and keep SEN support, EHCP and referral details out of the written report unless your school's policy says to include them; those belong in the review meetings with parents. If a pupil's attendance is a concern, your school's attendance process will already involve the family, so the report comment can stay on the learning that's been missed and how you're helping them catch up.

Common questions

How do you write a report card comment for a struggling student?

Start with a real, specific strength. Then describe the difficulty in words about what the student does (not labels), say what school is already doing about it, give the family one small thing to do at home, and invite them to get in touch. Keep it honest: a family shouldn't find out at the end of the year that their child was struggling all along.

Should I be honest on a report card if a student is failing?

Yes. Vague praise for a student who is failing leaves the family without the chance to help. Say it plainly once, alongside the support in place and what would change the grade, and make sure the family hears it from you by phone or in person before the report goes home.

What words should I avoid in report card comments?

Labels such as lazy, unmotivated, disruptive, careless or immature; absolutes such as always and never; comparisons with other students; vague lines such as "not working to potential"; and anything about a diagnosis, medication, a support plan or family circumstances, unless your school's policy says otherwise. Describe what happens and what helps instead.

Can I mention an IEP or 504 plan in a report card comment?

Follow your school's policy. Many schools keep plans, diagnoses and services out of the general report card comment and report on plan goals separately, in the plan's own progress reports and meetings. The comment can still describe what the student is working on and the support in class.

Can I use ChatGPT to write comments for struggling students?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names, diagnoses, support plans and family details. Give it short notes using a code for each student, ask for the strength, concern, support, home tip pattern, and then read every comment carefully: these are the comments families read most closely.
