Teacher Skills · Guide

Report card comments for science and social studies: 300+ examples by grade, and a free comment builder

Science and social studies comments are often the ones written last, at 11 pm, and it shows: "Leo enjoys science" tells a family nothing. This guide gives you comments for the skills that actually matter in each subject (asking questions, fair tests, measuring, explaining with evidence, timelines, maps, sources, causes and points of view) for grades K–2, 3–5 and 6–8, each with a strength, an on-track line, a next step and a home tip. There are also 22 rewrites of phrases to avoid, six complete comments, and a free builder that writes and checks each one, with UK wording for history and geography and an Ontario mode for progress reports and provincial report cards.

Updated 30 October 2026 · Grades K–8, with UK (history and geography) and Ontario wording · About an 8-minute read

The short version: comment on the skill, not the topic. Topics change every few weeks; skills carry on. "Leo plans fair tests, changing one thing and keeping everything else the same" says more than "Leo enjoyed our magnets unit". Open with one real strength, say what's on track, give one next step and how class is working on it ("using results to explain the answer, with 'I found that... because...' sentence starters"), and end with one small thing to try at home ("ask 'how do you know?' when he tells you a science fact"). Skip labels like "careless" or "not a science person", keep students' political or religious views out, and save safety incidents for a conversation. The builder below puts it together for either subject and checks the wording.

The pattern: skill, next step, home tip

PartWhat it doesExample
1. A specific strengthNames the skill, so the family knows what "good at science" means"Priya uses evidence from sources to support her points and judges how reliable they are."
2. On track (optional)Reassures the family about the part that's going fine"She places events in order and is learning to explain change and continuity, which is right on track for this point in the year."
3. Next step and how class helpsOne thing to aim for, with the support already in place"Our next step is explaining why something happened and what it led to, which we're practicing with cause-and-effect organizers."
4. Home tipOne small, everyday thing to do together, not a worksheet"At home, you could talk about a news story together and ask Priya what caused it and what might happen next."
5. CloseKeeps the door open"Please get in touch if you have any questions about Priya's progress in social studies."

A moment you noticed beats any stock phrase: "I was impressed when Leo suggested testing each paper towel with the same amount of water" tells the family you know their child, and it shows the skill (a fair test) without any jargon. One per comment is plenty.

Which skills to comment on

Both subjects have a short list of skills that run through every topic. They're what the standards are built around, so they're a safe thing to report on, but the standards' own names are teacher language. Here's how they translate:

The standards sayWrite in the comment
Asking questions and defining problems (NGSS practice 1)"asks questions that can be tested"
Planning and carrying out investigations (NGSS 3); "fair and comparative tests" (England, Years 3–4)"plans fair tests, changing one thing and keeping everything else the same"
Analyzing and interpreting data (NGSS 4); "taking accurate measurements using standard units""takes accurate measurements and records results clearly in tables"
Constructing explanations; engaging in argument from evidence (NGSS 6, 7). Many classes call this CER"backs up each conclusion with evidence from the results"
Developing and using models (NGSS 2)"uses labeled diagrams to explain how something works"
Developing questions and planning inquiries (C3 Framework, Dimension 1)"asks strong questions and follows them up with research"
Evaluating sources and using evidence (C3, Dimension 3)"asks who made a source, when and why, before using it as evidence"
Chronological understanding; change and continuity"explains what changed, what stayed the same and why"
Communicating conclusions and taking informed action (C3, Dimension 4)"presents her work clearly and answers questions about it"

The NGSS has eight science and engineering practices and the C3 Framework four dimensions (Dimension 2 covers civics, economics, geography and history). England's national curriculum lists "working scientifically" skills for each key stage; for Years 1–2 these are observing closely with simple equipment, performing simple tests, identifying and classifying, and gathering and recording data. Not every state uses the NGSS or the C3, so check yours.

Skills first, then name the topic

Families like to hear what their child learned about, and naming the topic helps them start a conversation ("what did you find out about volcanoes?"). But the topic alone isn't a comment. Use it to frame the skill: "In our forces unit, Leo planned a fair test of which surface slows a toy car most." Two cautions: topics change between drafting and printing, so check the comment still matches what was taught that term; and if your school uses one comment across several marking periods, a skill line ages better than a topic line.

Social studies: keep views and family stories out

  • Comment on how a student argues, not what they believe. "Supports a point of view with evidence and listens respectfully to others" is fair to every family. A student's political or religious views (or yours about theirs) don't belong on a report, even as praise.
  • Family history is the family's. Topics like migration, family trees and "my community" bring out personal stories. Praise the skill ("asked thoughtful questions when interviewing a grandparent"), not the details the student shared.
  • Debates are practice. If a student argued a side in a class debate, they may not hold that view. Report the skill: "argued a clear case and answered questions about it".

Science practicals: safety and behavior

A safety incident in a practical lesson (misusing equipment, ignoring instructions) belongs in a conversation with the family and your school's records, at the time it happened. By report time, the comment should say what comes next: "Our next step is following each step of a practical method carefully, with a method card." If behavior is the main story for this student, the behavior and work habits guide has the pattern and its own builder.

Middle school: grades, missing work and the student reading it

  • The grade says how; the comment says why. If a C is mostly a missing lab report or project, say which one, by when, and under your real late-work policy.
  • Write so the student can act on it. From about grade 6, students read their own report cards. "Backing up each conclusion with evidence from the data" is something a 12-year-old can do; "needs to think more scientifically" isn't.
  • Name the unit. "Cells and organisms" or "the American Revolution" tells the family what to ask about.
  • Point to help that exists. Office hours, revision guides, a homework club, the textbook's videos.

Common questions

What should a science report card comment include?

One specific skill the student is good at (asking questions, planning a fair test, measuring, explaining results with evidence), what's on track, one next step with how class is working on it, and one small thing to try at home. Name the topic if it helps, but comment on the skill.

What should a social studies report card comment include?

The same pattern, with social studies skills: using sources, timelines and change over time, maps and places, explaining causes and consequences, understanding different points of view, research and discussion. Comment on how the student argues and uses evidence, never on their political or religious views.

What words should I avoid in science and social studies comments?

Labels such as careless or lazy; fixed lines such as "not a science person" or "no interest in history"; guesses at feelings such as "bored"; comparisons with other students; classroom shorthand such as CER, variables, hypothesis or standard codes; "always" and "never"; and anything about a student's politics, religion or family story.

How do I write one line for a foundation subject on a UK primary report?

Pick the one skill that best sums up the term and write it as a strength plus a short next step: "Isla uses sources well to find out about the past; next, explaining why things changed." Set the builder's character limit to your box size.

How do I write Ontario science and social studies report card comments?

Describe a significant strength and a next step in language parents understand, and a way to help at home, without repeating the curriculum expectations or achievement chart wording (Growing Success, 2010). On the fall progress report, make the comment agree with the Progressing With Difficulty, Well or Very Well box. On the provincial report cards, say which strands are reported that term; in Grades 7 and 8, say if history or geography is not part of the program that term. The builder's Canada (Ontario) mode does this for you.

Can I use ChatGPT to write science and social studies comments?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you leave out names, diagnoses, support plans and family details. Give it short notes per student with a code instead of a name, ask for the strength, next step and home tip pattern, and read every comment before it goes on a report.
