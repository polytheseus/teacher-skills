Social-emotional report card comments: examples for every SEL skill, and a free comment builder

The social-emotional section is the hardest part of the report card to write. The grades are about behaviour and feelings, not work you can point to, and families read every word. Write "is shy" or "has trouble controlling his emotions" and it can sting for years; write "is a joy to have in class" and you've told them nothing. A good social-emotional comment describes something you have seen the child do, names one skill they are learning, and says how school (and, if you like, home) is helping. Below: wording for 16 skills in the five areas most US SEL report cards use, at three levels, how-we-help and home tips for each, 18 rewrites of phrases to avoid, six complete comments, and a free builder that writes and checks each comment in your character limit.

Updated 31 October 2026 · Grades K–8, with UK wording for Reception to Year 9 · About an 8-minute read

The short version: lead with a social or emotional strength you have seen ("notices how others are feeling and responds with real kindness"), then one skill the child is still learning, described as what they do now ("is learning ways to calm down when upset, such as taking deep breaths"), then one next step ("Next, Mateo is working on using one calming strategy at the first sign of feeling upset") and how school is helping. Leave out diagnoses and guesses about mental health, incidents ("hit another child"), home circumstances, labels ("shy", "bossy", "aggressive"), hurtful friendship lines ("has no friends"), comparisons and SEL jargon ("self-regulation", "the yellow zone"). If you're worried about a child, call the family; the report isn't the place to tell them. The builder below puts it together and checks it.

Before you write: six rules for social-emotional comments

  • Describe what you see, not what you think is going on inside. "Is learning to put her feelings into words and does this well when talking one to one" is something you've watched happen. "Is anxious" or "has low self-esteem" is a guess about a child's mind, and it can follow them.
  • Never diagnose, and never hint. ADHD, anxiety, autism, trauma, "sensory issues", "anger issues": even when a family has told you, the written report is the wrong place. The same goes for counseling, behavior plans, IEPs and 504s. Your school's support team and a conversation are the right route.
  • Keep incidents and home out. "Hit another child at recess" and "since the divorce" belong in your behavior system, a phone call or a meeting. The report comment is for the skill and the next step.
  • Be very careful with friendships. "Has no friends" or "is a loner" is one of the most painful things a family can read, and older children read their own reports. Say what helps instead: "is building friendships and is most comfortable with one friend or a small group".
  • Lead with a strength, even a small one. Every child shows at least one of the 16 skills below. If both skills you want to mention are still developing, find a third that isn't.
  • Use family words. "Self-regulation", "the yellow zone", "SEL competencies" and "restorative conversation" mean a lot in the staff room and very little at the kitchen table. Say what the child does: "uses a calming strategy with a reminder".

Social-emotional report card comments by skill and level

The skills are grouped under the five areas of the CASEL framework (self-awareness, self-management, social awareness, relationship skills and responsible decision-making), which most US SEL standards and report card sections are built on. Each skill has three levels: Consistently, Usually and Developing. Where younger and older children need different words, the grades 3–8 version is underneath.

Matching your report card's scale

Standards-based report cards grade social-emotional skills on a scale, and the words vary by district: 4–3–2–1, "Consistently / Sometimes / Rarely", "Secure / Developing / Beginning", or M / P / B. Use Consistently for the top of your scale, Usually for the middle and Developing for anything below. Two cautions: don't repeat the score in the comment (it's already on the report), and if your district's lowest band says "rarely" or "not yet", the comment is where you show the child is learning, not stuck.

When you're worried about a child

If a child seems withdrawn, very upset, unsafe or isolated, the report card is the wrong place to raise it, and it shouldn't be the first time the family hears. Talk to your school counselor, or in the UK your designated safeguarding lead, and follow your school's procedures; then call or meet the family. The comment can still be honest and kind: name a real strength, the skill being worked on, and how school is helping.

Common questions

What are some positive social-emotional report card comments?

Describe something you've seen: "Mateo notices how others are feeling and responds with real kindness", "Ava cooperates in group work and shares materials and ideas", "Sam takes responsibility for their actions and learns from mistakes". Then add one next step, even for a child who is strong, such as "Next, Ava is working on taking on different roles in a group, including leading it."

How do I write about a child who struggles to manage their emotions?

Describe what happens now and what helps, then one small next step: "Leo is learning ways to calm down when upset, such as taking slow breaths or a short break. Next, he is working on using one calming strategy at the first sign of feeling upset. We are practicing this at school with a calm-down plan that Leo helped to choose." Lead with a strength, leave out incidents and words like "meltdowns" or "anger issues", and talk to the family before the report goes home.

What should I write for a quiet or shy student?

Avoid "shy" as a label; being quiet isn't a problem in itself. Say what helps: "Ella is building friendships and is most comfortable with one friend or a small group. Next, she is working on joining in with a new group at recess." If the child seems isolated or unhappy, call the family rather than putting it in writing.

What are the five SEL areas on a report card?

Most US SEL standards follow the five CASEL areas: self-awareness (recognizing feelings and strengths), self-management (managing feelings, self-control, perseverance), social awareness (empathy, other points of view, respect), relationship skills (friendships, communication, teamwork, sorting out disagreements) and responsible decision-making (safe choices, taking responsibility, solving problems). Your district's report card may use its own names for them.

Should I mention a diagnosis like ADHD or anxiety in the social-emotional comment?

No, even if the family has told you. Describe what you see and what helps, and keep diagnoses, counseling, behavior plans, IEPs and 504s out of the written report unless your school's policy says otherwise. Talk to the family and your support team directly.

Can I use ChatGPT to write social-emotional report card comments?

Yes, within your school's AI policy, if you use a code or initial instead of names and leave out diagnoses, family details, incidents and support plans. Give it one strength and one skill to work on per child, ask for a next step and a line on how school is helping, and read every comment before it goes home.
