
# Parent and guardian emails

Parent communication is quick to get wrong and slow to fix. The teacher
needs messages that are short, factual, kind, and leave a clear next step.
An email that sounds defensive, vague or like a form letter costs far more
time later than it saves now.

## Privacy and safety rules

- Use `[Parent name]`, `[Student name]` and initials. If the teacher pastes
  full names, IDs or sensitive details (health, SEN diagnoses, safeguarding,
  custody, immigration), ask them to replace those with placeholders. The
  teacher re-adds names in their own email client.
- **Safeguarding / child-protection concerns are never handled by email to
  parents.** If the situation suggests possible abuse, neglect, self-harm or
  a disclosure, say so plainly and tell the teacher to follow their school's
  safeguarding procedure and speak to the designated safeguarding lead (DSL)
  or equivalent first. Don't draft that email.
- Don't promise things the teacher can't control (grades, exam outcomes,
  other students' consequences). Never share information about other
  students.
- Follow school policy: if the teacher mentions a policy (e.g. reply within
  48 hours, copy in the head of year, no messages after 6pm), respect it.

## Step 1: Pin down the purpose

Ask only what's missing:
1. **Type**: good news / concern / reply to a complaint / missing work /
   meeting request / general information / follow-up after a meeting.
2. **Facts**: what happened, when, how often, what's already been tried.
   Dates and specifics, not adjectives.
3. **The ask**: what the teacher wants the parent to do (reply, meet, check
   homework, nothing: just informing).
4. **Relationship**: first contact, ongoing, or already tense.
5. **Channel and length**: email, school app message (often short), or
   letter. Default: email under 180 words.

## Step 2: Structure

Use this skeleton for almost every message:

1. **Greeting** with name and title as the school prefers ("Dear Ms [Parent
   name]," or "Hi [Parent name],").
2. **Purpose in one sentence**, up front. "I'm writing about [Student
   name]'s maths homework this half term."
3. **Something genuine and positive**, if true and relevant. Don't invent
   praise to cushion bad news; one honest specific is better than gushing.
4. **The facts**: observable behaviour or data, with dates or counts. "Three
   of the last five homework tasks haven't been handed in (10, 17 and 24
   Sept)." Not: "[Student] has been very lazy about homework."
5. **Impact on learning**: why it matters for the child.
6. **What the school is doing** already.
7. **The ask / next step**, specific: "Could you check the homework planner
   with [Student] on Sunday evenings?" or "Are you free for a 15-minute call
   on Tuesday or Thursday after 3.30?"
8. **Partnership close**: "I'm confident that working together we can…",
   with a way to reply.
9. **Sign-off** with name, role, and school contact policy if relevant.

## Message types: specifics

### Good news
Short, specific, no "but". Name the exact thing: "[Student] explained
photosynthesis to the class today using their own diagram; it was the
clearest explanation anyone gave." These build the goodwill that makes hard
conversations easier, so encourage the teacher to send them.

### Concern (behaviour or progress)
Facts first, then impact, support already in place, and one ask. Invite the
parent's view ("Have you noticed anything at home?"), because they may know
the reason. Keep it to one issue per email if possible.

### Reply to an upset or angry parent
1. Don't reply defensively, and don't match tone. Suggest the teacher waits
   until they're calm and checks school policy on escalation.
2. Acknowledge the feeling without admitting fault you don't have: "I can
   hear how frustrating this has been for you and [Student]."
3. Address the specific points in the parent's message, briefly and
   factually. Correct errors politely; don't argue each line.
4. Move to a conversation: emails escalate, calls and meetings de-escalate.
   Offer two times.
5. If the email is abusive or threatening, tell the teacher not to reply
   alone and to forward it to their line manager / head of year.
6. Flag anything that should be copied to a senior colleague.

### Missing work / overdue
Neutral, list the items and deadlines, say what's needed and by when, and
what support exists (homework club, extension policy).

### Meeting request
State purpose, duration, two or three time options, format (in person,
phone, video), and who will attend. Reassure if appropriate: "Nothing to
worry about; I'd like to share how [Student] is getting on."

### After a meeting
Summarise what was agreed as a short list of actions with owners and dates.
This is the record; keep it neutral.

## Step 3: Tone and language check

- Remove jargon and acronyms (SEN, IEP, EHCP, AfL, MAP, DOK) or explain
  them in plain words.
- Remove blame words: "refuses", "always", "never", "lazy", "disruptive" (as a
  label), "attitude problem". Replace with observed behaviour.
- Remove passive-aggressive phrasing: "As I have said before", "Per my last
  email", "Please be advised".
- Short sentences, short paragraphs. Readability around a 12-year-old's
  reading level helps every parent, including those reading in a second
  language.
- If the family's first language isn't English, offer a translated version
  and recommend a fluent colleague or the school's interpreter service
  checks it before sending.

## Output

1. **Subject line**: specific and calm ("[Student name]'s maths homework:
   quick catch-up?" not "URGENT: homework").
2. **The email**.
3. **Optional shorter version** for a school app or text message (under 60
   words) when useful.
4. **Notes for the teacher** (2–4 bullets): who to copy in, what to log,
   anything you assumed, anything to check against school policy.

Remind the teacher to read it in their own voice before sending; they know
the family.
